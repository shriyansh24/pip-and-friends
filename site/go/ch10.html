CHAPTER 10

TWO WINDOWS, ONE SHELF

Picture: Scoot, the bike courier in a red cap and green jacket, shrugs in surprise.

Chapter 9 gave you windows onto a shelf. Here's the twist: two windows can look at the same shelf. Change a box through one and the other sees it. This chapter shows when slices share and when they don't, the famous append surprise, how to make a true copy, shelves of shelves, how to take a box out of the middle, and Go's ready-made slices toolbox for sorting and searching. About 100 minutes, with 25 hands-on checks.

  1. Two windows, one shelf: slices that share
  2. Same shelf or new shelf? append decides
  3. A true copy: copy and slices.Clone
  4. Shelves of shelves: slices of slices
  5. Taking a box out: deleting and inserting
  6. The slices toolbox: sort, search, compare
  7. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · TWO WINDOWS, ONE SHELFSlices that share a backing array

1

Scoot's route has five stops. Today's run covers three of them.

Scoot says: I sliced today's three stops out of my route with route[1:4], like chapter 9 taught me. Then I marked the first one done on today's list.

Scoot says: And now my whole route says done as well. I never touched route! What happened?

Picture: Scoot shrugs, worried, holding a letter.

2

Remember the picture: a slice is a window onto a shelf (a backing array). Slicing makes a new window, but not a new shelf. So now there are two windows looking at the same boxes.

  1. 1The window route: all five boxes.
  2. 2The window today := route[1:4]: boxes B, C and D of the same shelf.
  3. 3Paint box B through today, and route sees the new paint, because it's the same box.
  4. 4One shelf. Slicing never builds a second one.

Scoot says: Catchphrase: a new window is not a new shelf. Two slices on one shelf are called aliases: two names for the same boxes.

    PART 2 · SAME SHELF OR NEW SHELF?append decides, and you can't see it decide
    

  
    
    9
    
    Chapter 9: when the shelf is full, append builds a new, longer one. When there's room, it doesn't. So b := append(a, 4) comes in two flavours.
    
    
    
      
    
        
    ROOM LEFTlen(a) < cap(a)
    
        
    The 4 goes in the next spare box of the same shelf. b is a wider window onto a's shelf. They share.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    SHELF FULLlen(a) == cap(a)
    
        
    Go builds a new shelf, copies a's boxes across, adds the 4. b looks at the new shelf. They don't share.
    
      
    
    
    
    
    Pictures: the signpost creature with arms reading room and full; the crate creature, a fresh row of boxes holding 0, 0, 0 and 4.
    
    
    
      
      
    Scoot says: Same line of code, two different results. Which one you get depends on cap, not on anything you can see in the line.

        A preview of a power tool. A slice expression may have a third number: s[low:high:max]. The third number says where the window's capacity stops, so append can't reach past it.
        
    
        
      a[0low:3high:3max · cap stops]

          3. 
      
        3. A window whose cap equals its len is full, so the next append must build a new shelf. No sharing.

          PART 3 · A TRUE COPYcopy, and slices.Clone
          

  
          
    16
    
          Sharing is great until you want two separate lists. Then you need a second shelf and the boxes copied onto it. The built-in copy(dst, src) copies boxes from src (source) into dst (destination).
          
    
          
    
            
      
          1. Destination first, source second. Think dst = src: the arrow points left, like an assignment.
            2. 
      
          2. copy never grows dst. It copies as many boxes as both windows show: the smaller of the two lengths.
            3. 
      
          3. It gives back that number, so you can check how many boxes were copied.
            4. 
    
          
  
          

  
          
    17
    
          The recipe for a true copy: build a destination as long as the source, then copy.
          
    copy_basic.go
    
          
    
          
    
            
      
          1. make([]int, 3) (line 7)
            A new shelf showing three zeros. Length 3, so there are three boxes to fill.
            2. 
      
          2. n := copy(dst, src) (line 8)
            Three boxes copied; n is 3.
            3. 
      
          3. [1 2 3] [99 2 3]
            Painting dst[0] left src alone. Two shelves, two lists.
            4. 
    
          
  
          

  
          
    18
    
          Feed the copy machine. Here src is always []int{1, 2, 3}; only the length of dst changes. What comes out is the count.
          
    
          
    
  
          

  
          
    19
    
    
          The classic copy trap. No error, and no copy either.
          
    copy_trap.go
    
          
    
          
    
          
      
      
          Scoot says: var dst []int is a nil slice: length 0. copy doesn't grow anything, so 0 boxes are copied. The 0 at the start of the line is copy telling you so.
          
    
          
    
          
      
          Scoot says: Fix: dst := make([]int, len(src)). The destination needs length, not just capacity: make([]int, 0, 3) copies nothing too.
          
      
    
          
    
          Pictures: the sneak critter beside the program; Scoot pointing at the fix.

    21
    
          Detective's cheat sheet. For each line: does the new slice share the old one's shelf?
          
    
            
      
          1. t := s[1:3] · shares. Slicing makes a window, never a shelf.
            2. 
      
          2. t := s · shares. Only the ticket is copied.
            3. 
      
          3. f(s) · shares. The function gets a copy of the ticket.
            4. 
      
          4. t := append(s, x) with room · shares. The surprise of panel 11.
            5. 
      
          5. t := append(s, x) when full · separate. A new, longer shelf.
            6. 
      
          6. append(s[:n:n], x) · separate. The window is full by design.
            7. 
      
          7. copy into make([]T, len(s)), or slices.Clone(s) · separate. A true copy.
            8. 
    
          
    
          
      
      
          Scoot says: Catchphrase: want your own list? Clone it. Sharing is fast and often exactly right; just never share by accident.
          
    
          
  
          

  
  
          PART 4 · SHELVES OF SHELVESSlices of slices: grids and tables
          

  
          
    22
    
          A box can hold anything, even another slice. A slice whose boxes are slices is a slice of slices, written [][]string. Picture a bookcase: the outer slice holds the shelves, each shelf holds the books.
          
    
    
          
      
      
          Scoot says: Two indexes, read in order: board[1][2] means "shelf 1, then box 2 on that shelf".
          
    
          
  
          

  
          
    23
    
          A game board as a [][]string. One literal per row, inside one big literal.
          
    grid.go

            Inside the outer braces, each row is written as just {"X", "O", "X"}: Go already knows every box is a []string. The comma after the last row is required when the closing brace is on its own line.

            Row 1, box 2: the middle row's last dot becomes O.
            3.

            Each lap hands you one whole row, a []string.
            4.

            len(board) counts rows; len(board[0]) counts the boxes in row 0.

            PART 5 · TAKING A BOX OUTDeleting and inserting
            

  
            
    27
    
            Go has no "delete" on a slice built into the language. You take box 2 out by sliding every later box one place to the left, then making the window one box narrower. One line does both:
            
    
            
      s = append(s[:2]keep: boxes 0, 1,s[3:]...slide in: box 3 on)
    
            
    
              
      
            1. s[:2] is the part before the box to remove. Its capacity reaches the end of the shelf, so there's room.
              2. 
      
            2. s[3:]... is everything after it, passed box by box (the ... from panel 20).
              3. 
      
            3. Because there's room, append writes those boxes onto the same shelf, straight over box 2.
              4.

              Chapter 9 promised it: slices.Equal(a, b) is the ready-made version of the box-by-box loop you wrote, because a == b doesn't compile for slices.
              
    
                
      
              1. Same length and the same value in every box, in the same order: true.
                2. 
      
              2. Same values in a different order: false. Order matters.
                3. 
      
              3. A nil slice and an empty one count as equal: both have no boxes.

              What you learned in Chapter 10
              
    
                
      
              • A NEW WINDOW IS NOT A NEW SHELFSlicing, t := s and passing s to a function all share the backing array. Paint through one, the other sees it.
                • 
      
              • APPEND WITH ROOM SHARES; APPEND WHEN FULL MOVESYou can't see which from the line. That's the [0 0 0 5] [0 0 0 5] surprise. A full slice expression s[:n:n] forces a move.
                • 
      
              • COPY FILLS ONLY THE BOXES YOU HAVEcopy(dst, src) copies the smaller length and returns the count. A zero-length dst gets nothing.
                • 
      
              • WANT YOUR OWN LIST? CLONE IT.slices.Clone(s), or make + copy.
                • 
      
              • SHELVES OF SHELVES NEED THEIR SHELVES[][]T: make the outer slice, then give every row boxes before indexing it.
                • 
      
              • SORT AND REVERSE WORK IN PLACEThey return nothing. Contains, Index, Max, Equal answer questions; Delete and Insert hand back a slice to catch.

    Under the hood: headers, growslice and memmove
    
              
      
              
        
        
              The Professor says: Every surprise in this chapter falls out of one fact: a slice value is three words, and only those three words are ever copied.
              
      
              
      
              The header, again
              
      
              A slice value is a small struct the runtime source calls slice: a pointer to an element of a backing array, a length and a capacity (24 bytes on a 64-bit machine). Assignment, passing to a function, returning from one, storing in another slice or a struct: all of these copy those 24 bytes. None of them copy elements. The language specification puts it this way: a slice "is a descriptor for a contiguous segment of an underlying array", and it "shares storage with its array and with other slices of the same array".
              
      
              The append decision, exactly
              
      
              For append(s, x...) the spec says: if the capacity of s is not large enough to fit the additional values, append allocates a new, sufficiently large underlying array; otherwise, append re-uses the underlying array. In the runtime that's a single comparison, len(s) + n <= cap(s). When it fails, runtime.growslice picks a new capacity (roughly doubling small slices and growing big ones by smaller steps, rounded up to an allocator size class; the policy is in runtime/slice.go and has changed between releases), allocates, and copies the old elements across. That is why panel 10's append_full.go printed cap 6: the language promises "enough", and this runtime chose 6.
              
      
              copy is memmove
              
      
              copy(dst, src) copies min(len(dst), len(src)) elements and returns that count. Under the hood it's a block move (memmove), which is why it's fast and why it handles overlap correctly: copy(s[1:], s) shifts everything right by one without smearing the first element across the slice. The append(s[:i], s[i+1:]...) delete is the same kind of overlapping move, done by append. A special case worth knowing: copy also accepts a string as its source when the destination is a []byte (chapter 11's notebook runs one).
              
      
              The full slice expression's rules
              
      
              a[low:high:max] needs 0 <= low <= high <= max <= cap(a). The result has length high - low and capacity max - low. Only low may be left out. It works on arrays, pointers to arrays and slices, but not on strings (a string has no spare capacity to protect).
              
      
              Why slices.Delete zeroes the tail
              
      
              Since Go 1.22, slices.Delete, DeleteFunc, Compact, CompactFunc and Replace clear the elements between the new length and the old one. If those boxes held pointers (to big strings, structs or other slices), leaving stale copies behind the wall would keep that memory alive: the garbage collector can't know nobody will ever re-slice up to them. The hand-written append(s[:i], s[i+1:]...) doesn't clear anything, which is panel 28's leftover e.
              
      
              A slice of slices is not a 2D array
              
      
              [][]int is an outer header pointing at an array of inner headers, each pointing at its own row array, possibly far apart in memory and of different lengths. A true fixed grid, [3][3]int, is one block of 9 ints. Effective Go's "Two-dimensional slices" section shows a third option: allocate one big []int and slice the rows out of it, so all rows share one array.
              
    
              
  
              

  
              
    In real programs: where sharing helps, and where it bites
    
              
      
                
        
              • Sub-slices without copying. Parsers and network code take windows like buf[:n] or line[start:end] all the time. Sharing makes them free, with no copying of megabytes.
                • 
        
              • Functions that fill a slice you pass in. io.Reader's Read(p []byte) (chapter 19) writes into your buffer through its copy of the header. That's panel 7's stamp, at industrial scale.
                • 
        
              • Returning a slice from a function. The standard pattern is s = f(s), exactly like append, for anything that might grow the slice.
                • 
        
              • Defensive copies. A type that stores a slice someone handed it often clones it first (slices.Clone), so the caller can't change its contents later by accident. The same goes for returning internal slices.
                • 
        
              • Holding a big array alive. A tiny window like big[:2] keeps the whole backing array in memory. When keeping a small piece of a big buffer for a long time, clone the piece (100 Go Mistakes, #26).
                • 
        
              • In-place filtering with kept := s[:0] (Boss 4) is common in performance-sensitive code; slices.DeleteFunc does the same job with a function you supply (chapter 17).
                • 
        
              • Sorting. slices.Sort for plain values; sort.Strings, sort.Ints and sort.Slice in older code; slices.SortFunc for "sort people by age" (chapter 17). slices.BinarySearch finds a value quickly in an already-sorted slice.
                • 
        
              • Grids. Game boards, spreadsheets, images and matrices are usually [][]T, built with the two-step make from panel 25.

    Common wrong ideas
    
              
      
                
        
              • "t := s makes a copy of the list." It copies the ticket. Use slices.Clone(s) for a copy.
                • 
        
              • "s[1:3] is a new, separate slice." It's a new window on the same shelf. Changes show through.
                • 
        
              • "append always returns a fresh slice." Only when the shelf is full. With room, the result shares, which is panel 11's surprise.
                • 
        
              • "append(a, x) can't change anything a can see." True for a's own boxes; but it can overwrite a spare box another slice (like b) is already showing.
                • 
        
              • "copy grows the destination." Never. It fills at most len(dst) boxes and returns how many.
                • 
        
              • "make([]T, 0, n) is a fine destination for copy." Length 0: nothing is copied. Use make([]T, len(src)).
                • 
        
              • "A function can't change my slice." It can change the boxes (shared shelf). It can't change your length, unless you catch a returned slice.
                • 
        
              • "Deleting with append(s[:i], s[i+1:]...) leaves the original alone." It slides boxes on the shared shelf; the original is changed.
                • 
        
              • "sorted := slices.Sort(s)." Sort works in place and returns nothing: a compile error.
                • 
        
              • "Sorting strings is alphabetical." It's by character code: capitals before small letters.
                • 
        
              • "make([][]int, 3) makes a 3-by-something grid." It makes 3 nil rows. Each row needs its own make or append.
                • 
        
              • "slices.Max of an empty slice is 0." It panics: slices.Max: empty list.
                • 
        
              • "If I drop a slices.Delete result, something will warn me." The Compiler won't: the spot-the-bug program after panel 30 builds and runs. Go's checker go vet (chapter 2's Specs) does warn. Here it is on the same program, saved as vet_delete.go:

    Words from this chapter
    
              
      
              
        
              backing array
              The array (the shelf) a slice looks onto. Several slices can share one.
              
        
              alias
              A second name for the same thing. Two slices on one shelf are aliases for the boxes they both show.
              
        
              aliasing bug
              A bug where changing one slice unexpectedly changes another, because they share a shelf.
              
        
              slice header
              The three-part value in a slice variable: pointer, length, capacity (chapter 9's ticket). It's what gets copied.
              
        
              reallocation
              What append does when the shelf is full: build a bigger one and copy the boxes across.
              
        
              copy(dst, src)
              Built-in that copies min(len(dst), len(src)) elements from src into dst and returns that count.
              
        
              destination, source
              Where a copy goes (dst) and where it comes from (src).
              
        
              independent copy, clone
              A slice with its own backing array holding the same values; changes to one never show in the other.
              
        
              slices.Clone
              Returns an independent copy of a slice.
              
        
              ... (spread)
              After a slice in a call, s... passes its elements one by one, as in append(a, b...).
              
        
              full slice expression
              s[low:high:max]: a slice whose capacity stops at max.
              
        
              slice of slices
              A slice whose elements are slices, like [][]string: a grid or table.
              
        
              jagged
              A slice of slices whose rows have different lengths.
              
        
              in place
              Changing the elements where they already are, instead of building a new slice. slices.Sort and slices.Reverse work in place.
              
        
              slices package
              Standard-library package (Go 1.21+) with Sort, Contains, Index, Reverse, Max, Min, Equal, Clone, Delete, Insert and more.
              
        
              sort.Strings
              Older standard-library function that sorts a []string in place.
              
        
              preallocate
              Give a slice its final capacity up front, make([]T, 0, n), so append never moves it.
              
        
              memmove
              A fast block copy of memory that handles overlapping source and destination; what copy uses.
