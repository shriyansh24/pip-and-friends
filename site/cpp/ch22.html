C++ · CHAPTER 22

KEY CARDS, MOVING DAY

Picture: Wren, the mechanic in orange coveralls, waves hello. Next to her stands Basil, a hotel concierge in a burgundy jacket with gold buttons and a pillbox hat, holding out a gold key card.

Wren is spending a week at the Hotel Ferrum, and the concierge, Basil, runs the place with one rule: the room is yours from check-in until check-out, whichever door you leave by. In C++ that rule has a clumsy name, RAII, and it is the reason good C++ code almost never says "close", "unlock" or "delete". Then it's moving day. Copying a house brick by brick is slow, so C++ lets you hand over the keys and the truck instead: std::move, move constructors and the strange, empty house left behind. About 130 minutes, with 24 hands-on checks.

  1. Check-in, check-out: resources, and the key card that is the room (RAII)
  2. Every exit leads to check-out: early returns, exceptions, and destructors that must not throw
  3. A key card for every door: files, unique_ptr with a deleter, locks and scope guards
  4. Moving day: lvalues, rvalues, T&& and what std::move really does
  5. Writing the movers: move constructor, move assignment, noexcept, elision and sink parameters
  6. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · CHECK-IN, CHECK-OUTresources, and the key card that is the room

1

Wren rings the bell at the front desk. Basil slides a gold card across the counter. "Room 12. The card is the room: while you hold it, nobody else gets the room. And when you check out, by the front door, the fire escape or the laundry chute, the card comes back to me and the room is cleaned for the next guest. You never have to remember."

Basil says: Every guest who checks in, checks out. Every exit leads to check-out. That's not a promise I make. It's how the building is built.

Picture: Basil the concierge rings his desk bell and points at the timeline.

2

A resource is anything a program borrows from the computer and must give back. Chapter 13 met the first one: heap memory, borrowed with new and returned with delete. There are many more, and every one has its own "check in" and "check out" step.

Wren says: "Every bolt you tighten, you loosen later." Forget to loosen it and nothing breaks today. But a program that forgets to close files runs out of them; one that forgets to unlock a lock leaves the next person waiting forever. These bugs don't crash. They just pile up.

    4
    
The fix is a class. Its constructor checks in (acquires the resource), and its destructor checks out (releases it). Chapter 12 showed that C++ runs a destructor automatically when an object's life ends, at the closing brace of its scope. So the clean-up happens exactly once, on every way out, without anyone remembering.

    

    

      
THE NAMERAII
"Resource Acquisition Is Initialization": getting the resource is making the object. It's a clumsy name for a simple idea, so many people say scope-bound resource management: the resource is bound to a scope.

      
THE RULECore Guideline R.1
"Manage resources automatically using resource handles and RAII." A resource handle is the object that owns the resource, like a key card owns a room.

    

    

      
      
Basil says: The card is the room. Hold the card, have the room. Lose the card, at the end of its scope, and the room is handed back. No separate "remember to check out" step exists at all.

    Why newest first? Because a later object may depend on an earlier one. Think of a lift key (the lock) and the room on the top floor you reach with it: you leave the room before you hand back the lift key, never the other way round. So it locks and unlocks before the work it was meant to protect. This little Guard prints instead of locking, so you can see it.

    PART 4 · MOVING DAYlvalues, rvalues, T&& and what std::move really does
    

  
    
    18
    
    
    End of the week, and Wren is moving into a new workshop. She could copy her old one: buy a second sofa, a second piano, forty more boxes of books, and build it all again. Or she could move: hand the new place the keys and the furniture truck. The old workshop is still standing afterwards, but it's empty.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    Wren says: A std::string or a std::vector is exactly like my workshop: a small object on the stack holding the keys (a pointer) to a big block on the heap. Copying builds a second block. Moving just hands over the pointer. The furniture never moves at all.

    moving_day.cpp
    
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    THIS LIBRARY'S CHOICE: the empty [] on the last line is what libstdc++ leaves in a moved-from string. The C++ standard only promises "valid but unspecified": some other library could leave something else. Panel 24 says what that means.
    
  
    

  
    
    20
    
    How does C++ know when moving is safe? It looks at what kind of thing you hand over. The simple view: an lvalue has a name and lives on, like a house with a street address; you'll want it again, so it must be copied. An rvalue is a temporary, like a pop-up tent that's gone tonight; nobody can ever look at it again, so taking its insides is free.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    The Professor says: The names are history: an lvalue could stand on the left of =, an rvalue only on the right. A quick test that works most of the time: if you can write & in front of it and get an address, it's an lvalue. The full rules have five categories; the notebook lists them, and you won't need them today.
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    21

With both overloads written (lines 5 and 9), C++ picks the && one for every rvalue.

Valid: it is still a proper string or vector, and every operation that needs nothing particular from its value works (your own classes should promise the same: Core Guideline C.64).     29
    
      Why do all these move constructors say noexcept? Because of std::vector. When a vector runs out of room, it borrows a bigger block and must carry every element across. Moving is cheap, but if a move threw halfway, the elements already moved would be ruined, and the vector promised it never loses your data. So it moves only when the move promises never to throw. Otherwise it copies. (emplace_back() builds each item right inside the vector, so only the carrying across is counted.)
      
    vector_grow.cpp
    
      
      
      
      
      
    
      
    
      THIS TOOLCHAIN: 3 because libstdc++'s vector grows from room for 1 to 2 to 4 (carrying 1, then 2). The noexcept rule itself is standard; the exact counts are not.
      
    
      
      
      
      Wren says: Same class, same moves, and Unsure got copied every time, just for missing one word. For a vector of big strings that's the difference between handing over keys and rebuilding houses. Rule (C.66): make move operations noexcept. They almost never need to throw: they only shuffle pointers. And reserve, when you know the size, avoids the carrying entirely.

Now t is no longer "just a local name", the elision rule doesn't apply, and a real move happens.

      What you learned in Chapter 22
      
    
        
      
      • EVERY BOLT YOU TIGHTEN, YOU LOOSEN LATERA resource is anything borrowed that must be given back: memory, files, locks, connections. Manual clean-up is skipped by every early return, break and exception (panel 3).
        • 
      
      • THE CARD IS THE ROOMRAII: the constructor acquires, the destructor releases, and the resource lives exactly as long as the object. Objects in one scope are destroyed newest first. The standard library's key cards: std::string, std::vector, std::fstream, std::unique_ptr (with a deleter for C resources), std::scoped_lock.
        • 
      
      • EVERY EXIT LEADS TO CHECK-OUTNormal end, early return, continue or exception: the destructor runs, and during unwinding it runs before the catch. A guard must have a name, or it dies at the semicolon. Destructors must never throw: that calls std::terminate.
        • 
      
      • HAND OVER THE KEYS, NOT THE HOUSEMoving steals a heap block instead of copying it. T&& binds only to rvalues (temporaries). std::move is only a cast, a permission slip; the move constructor or move assignment does the taking and must leave the old object empty but valid.
        • 
      
      • DON'T READ, ONLY REFILLA moved-from standard object is valid but unspecified: assign, clear or destroy it, never trust its value. Moving a const object silently copies.
        • 
      
      • PROMISE, AND DON'T GET IN THE WAYMark moves noexcept so std::vector uses them. Return locals by name: return std::move(local); blocks elision. Take sink parameters by value and move them in. Best of all, write no special members (rule of zero).

    Under the hood: value categories, what std::move is, what the compiler generates, and why vector checks noexcept
    
      
      
      The five value categories
      
      
      The comic used two words. The standard uses five, arranged as a small family tree. Every expression is either a glvalue ("generalised lvalue": it names an object that has an identity) or a prvalue ("pure rvalue": a value with no object yet, like 7, a + b or Truck{}). Glvalues split into lvalues (named things, like house) and xvalues ("expiring": an object with an identity that you may take from, which is exactly what std::move(house) produces). Xvalues and prvalues together are the rvalues, and that's the group T&& binds to. One surprise: inside a function, a parameter declared std::string&& s is itself an lvalue, because it has a name. To pass it on as an rvalue you need std::move(s) again.
      
      
      What std::move really is
      
      
      In libstdc++ it's one line: a function template that returns static_cast<std::remove_reference_t<T>&&>(t). No code runs, nothing is copied or cleared. It is marked [[nodiscard]] and noexcept, which is why panel 23 got a warning. Its cousin std::forward is used in templates that pass arguments on unchanged ("perfect forwarding"), which this book leaves for later reading.
      
      
      When the compiler writes the special members
      
      
        
        
      • The move constructor and move assignment are generated only if you declare none of these yourself: copy constructor, copy assignment, move assignment or constructor, destructor. That's Boss 6: an empty destructor switched the moves off.
        • 
        
      • The copy members are still generated in that case (an old rule kept for compatibility, now "deprecated"), unless you declare a move member: then the copies are deleted.
        • 
        
      • A generated move moves each member in declaration order, and is noexcept when all the members' moves are. A raw pointer member is just copied by it, which is why Truck needed its own.
        • 
        
      • = default asks for the generated version explicitly; = delete forbids one. Both count as "declared".
        • 
      
      
      
      Why std::vector checks noexcept
      
      
      push_back promises the strong guarantee: if it throws, the vector is exactly as before. When it grows, it uses std::move_if_noexcept on each element: a move if the move constructor is noexcept (or if there's no copy constructor at all, in which case it moves and gives up the guarantee), a copy otherwise. Copies leave the old block untouched, so a throw halfway can simply throw the new block away. Chapter 27 names these guarantees properly.
      
      
      Copy elision, exactly
      
      
        
        
      • Guaranteed (C++17): initialising an object from a prvalue of the same type, including return Truck{}; and Truck t = Truck{};. There's only one object from the start.
        • 
        
      • Allowed (NRVO): return t; for a local t of the return type. g++ and clang do it whenever every return returns the same local. -fno-elide-constructors turns it off in g++, if you want to see the moves.
        • 
        
      • Implicit move: where elision doesn't happen, return t; still treats t as an rvalue (Boss 4).
        • 
      
      
      
      How unique_ptr stores a deleter
      
      
      std::unique_ptr<FILE, CloseFile> keeps a CloseFile object inside it. Because CloseFile has no data, libstdc++ stores it in zero bytes, so the whole thing is still the size of one pointer. Writing std::unique_ptr<FILE, decltype(&std::fclose)> also works on most compilers, but stores a function pointer (twice the size), and taking the address of a standard library function is not something the standard promises to support. The struct is the portable choice.
      
      
      A home-made scope guard
      
      
      Without <experimental/scope>, ten lines do it: template <typename F> class ScopeExit { public: explicit ScopeExit(F f) : f_{std::move(f)} {} ~ScopeExit() { f_(); } ScopeExit(const ScopeExit&) = delete; ScopeExit& operator=(const ScopeExit&) = delete; private: F f_; };. Use it as ScopeExit sign{[] { ... }};. Real libraries add a release() function to cancel the job, and variants that run only on success or only on failure (scope_success, scope_fail in the same header).
      
    
      
  
      

  
      
    In real programs
    
      
      
        
        
      • Almost no delete, close or unlock. In modern C++ code bases these words appear inside a handful of small handle classes and nowhere else. Code reviewers treat a bare new or fopen outside such a class as a bug waiting to happen.
        • 
        
      • Wrapping C libraries. Operating-system and C library resources (sockets, database connections, graphics contexts, compression streams) come with "create" and "destroy" functions. Projects wrap each pair in a unique_ptr with a deleter, or a small move-only class like Boss 3's key card.
        • 
        
      • Locks. Every serious multi-threaded C++ program locks through std::scoped_lock, std::lock_guard or std::unique_lock (Chapter 29), never with bare lock()/unlock() pairs.
        • 
        
      • Transactions. Database libraries offer a transaction object whose destructor rolls back (undoes) unless commit() was called: an exception halfway leaves the data untouched. It's a scope guard with a cancel button.
        • 
        
      • Moves you get for free. Returning a std::vector or std::string from a function costs nothing extra, thanks to elision and moves. Before C++11, people passed output parameters by reference to avoid copies; today, return by value (Core Guideline F.20).
        • 
        
      • Containers of move-only things. std::vector<std::unique_ptr<Shape>> is everywhere (Chapter 24 uses it), and only works because vectors move their elements.
        • 
        
      • Sink constructors. Classes that store strings take them by value and move them in: explicit Guest(std::string name) : name_{std::move(name)} {}. You'll see it in nearly every modern code base.
        • 
      
      
    
      
  
      

  
      
    Common wrong ideas
    
      
      
        
        
      • "RAII is about memory." Memory is one resource of many: files, locks, connections, even "put the CLEAN sign up" (panel 17).
        • 
        
      • "I must call the destructor myself." Never for ordinary objects: C++ calls it at the end of the scope (panel 5). Calling it by hand as well would destroy the object twice.
        • 
        
      • "Exceptions skip the clean-up." They skip manual clean-up. Destructors run during unwinding, before the catch (panel 9).
        • 
        
      • "Guard{m}; and Guard g{m}; are the same." The first is a temporary that dies at the semicolon (panel 16).
        • 
        
      • "A destructor may throw if it has to." It calls std::terminate (panel 12).
        • 
        
      • "std::move moves." It casts. The receiver moves, or nobody does (panel 23).
        • 
        
      • "A moved-from object is destroyed" or "is always empty." It lives until its brace and its destructor still runs; standard types promise only "valid but unspecified" (panels 24 and 26).
        • 
        
      • "Adding std::move makes code faster." On a return of a local it makes it slower (panel 30); on a const object it does nothing but copy (panel 31).
        • 
        
      • "noexcept is just documentation." std::vector reads it and copies instead of moving without it (panel 29).
        • 
        
      • "An empty destructor is harmless." It switches off the generated moves (Boss 6).
        • 
        
      • "Moving is always cheap." Moving a std::array<int, 1000> copies all 1000 ints: there's no heap block to hand over. Moves are cheap for types that hold their contents elsewhere, like strings and vectors. (Very short strings are kept inside the string object itself, so moving them copies a few letters too.)

    Words from this chapter
    
      
      
      
        
      resource
      Anything a program borrows that must be given back: heap memory, an open file, a lock, a network connection.
      
        
      leak
      A resource that was borrowed and never given back.
      
        
      RAII
      Resource Acquisition Is Initialization: the constructor acquires a resource and the destructor releases it, so the resource lives exactly as long as the object. Also called scope-bound resource management.
      
        
      resource handle
      An object that owns a resource and releases it in its destructor, such as std::ofstream or std::unique_ptr.
      
        
      scope
      The part of the code between a pair of braces where a name exists; local objects die at its closing brace.
      
        
      reverse order of construction
      The order in which C++ destroys the objects of one scope: newest first.
      
        
      stack unwinding
      Destroying the local objects of each scope an exception leaves, on its way to a matching catch.
      
        
      flush
      Write out the data a stream has collected in its buffer.
      
        
      buffer
      A block of memory where a stream collects data before writing it in one big piece.
      
        
      deleter
      The function object a unique_ptr calls instead of delete, for example one that calls std::fclose.
      
        
      mutex / lock
      An object that lets only one part of a program at a time pass; std::mutex.
      
        
      lock guard
      A key card for a mutex: std::scoped_lock or std::lock_guard locks in its constructor and unlocks in its destructor.
      
        
      temporary
      An unnamed object made for one statement; it is destroyed at the end of that statement.
      
        
      scope guard
      An object that runs a given job when its scope ends, such as std::experimental::scope_exit.
      
        
      lambda
      A small unnamed function written in place: [] { ... }.
      
        
      noexcept
      A promise that a function never throws. Breaking it calls std::terminate. Destructors are noexcept by default.
      
        
      std::terminate
      Ends the program at once (on this system with Aborted and exit code 134).
      
        
      lvalue
      An expression that names an object that lives on, like a variable: you can take its address.
      
        
      rvalue
      A temporary, or an object marked as "may be taken from" by std::move.
      
        
      rvalue reference
      T&&: a reference that binds only to rvalues.
      
        
      std::move
      A cast to an rvalue reference: permission to take. It moves nothing by itself.
      
        
      move constructor
      T(T&& other): builds a new object by taking other's insides, leaving other empty but valid.
      
        
      move assignment
      T& operator=(T&& other): gives back the object's old resources, then takes other's.
      
        
      moved-from
      An object whose insides were taken. Standard types leave it valid but unspecified.
      
        
      valid but unspecified
      Still a proper object that can be assigned, cleared or destroyed, but holding a value you can't rely on.
      
        
      self-move
      Moving an object into itself, b = std::move(b);.
      
        
      copy elision
      Building an object directly in its final place, so no copy or move happens. Guaranteed for returned temporaries since C++17.
      
        
      NRVO
      Named return value optimisation: eliding the copy or move when a function returns a named local.
      
        
      pessimisation
      A change meant to speed code up that slows it down, like return std::move(local);.
      
        
      sink parameter
      A parameter whose value the function keeps; take it by value and move it into place.
      
        
      capacity
      How many elements a vector has room for before it must borrow a bigger block.
      
        
      emplace_back
      Builds a new element directly inside a vector, from constructor arguments.
      
        
      prvalue / xvalue / glvalue
      The standard's finer value categories: a pure value with no object yet, an expiring object, and anything with an identity.
