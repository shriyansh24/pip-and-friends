C++ · CHAPTER 13

STICKY NOTES, LIBRARY CARDS

Picture: Wren, the mechanic in orange coveralls, waves hello. Next to her: a sticky note with a house address and an arrow to the house, and a library card tucked into a book.

A pointer is a sticky note with an address on it. It isn't the house; it tells you where the house is. You can rewrite the note, leave it blank, or (careful!) keep it after the house has been knocked down. Then the library: things that live on the heap must be handed back when you're done, and std::unique_ptr is a borrower card that does the handing back for you. One book, one card, and when the card goes, the book goes home. About 120 minutes, with 24 hands-on checks.

  1. Sticky notes: addresses, & and *
  2. Rewrite it or rub it out: reseating and nullptr
  3. Notes for whole houses: -> and observers
  4. Demolished houses: dangling pointers
  5. The library card: the heap and std::unique_ptr
  6. Why not just new and delete?
  7. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · STICKY NOTESEvery box has an address, and a pointer is a note that holds one

1

Think of your computer's memory as one very long street. Every box your program makes (every int, every double, every object) lives in a house on that street, and every house has a number: its address.

Wren says: Up to now we used a box by its name: score. But the machine doesn't think in names. It thinks in house numbers. Today we learn to write a house number on a sticky note, carry the note around, and follow it back to the house. Close to the metal. Careful with the wrench.

Picture: Wren points at a street of numbered houses. One house holds the box score, with 42 inside.

    3
    
Big idea: a pointer is a box whose job is to hold an address. Write int* note{&score}; and read it right to left: "note is a pointer to an int, starting at the address of score". The * after the type turns "an int" into "a note saying where an int lives". The type matters: an int* note may only point at int houses.

    
    

      
Wren says: The note and the house are two different boxes. Throw the note away and the house is fine. Repaint the house and the note still says the same number. The note is not the house.

      
    

  


  

    4
    
Having a note is only useful if you can follow it. Put * in front of a pointer and you're standing in the house it points at. Line 8 reads the house through the note. Line 9 writes into it. Following a pointer is called dereferencing it.

    5
    
Confusing on purpose? A bit. C++ reuses * and & for two jobs each. The trick: next to a type (when you make a new name) they describe the kind of box. In an expression (when you use a name) they do something.

    

      
NEXT TO A TYPEint* p
"p is a pointer to int": a note that holds an int's address.

      
IN AN EXPRESSION*p
"follow p": the house p points at. Read it or write it.

      
NEXT TO A TYPEint& r
"r is a nickname (a reference, Chapter 8) for an int": a second name glued to a box.

      
IN AN EXPRESSION&x
"the address of x": the house number, ready to write on a note.

    

    
Four cards. Solid border: next to a type, int-star p makes a pointer and int-ampersand r makes a nickname. Dashed border: in an expression, star p follows the pointer and ampersand x gives the address.

    

      
      
Wren says: And they undo each other: & turns a house into a house number; * turns a house number back into the house. So *&score is just score. & makes the note; * follows it. (Spacing doesn't matter to the Compiler: int* p, int *p and int * p mean the same. This book glues the star to the type.)

    21
    
          Notes are cheap and useful, and every bug in this part came from one of three ways a note can lie. Wren's rules for keeping them honest:
          
    
            
      
          1. Every note holds a real address or nullptr. Never leave one unwritten (panel 14).
            2. 
      
          2. Check before you follow whenever nullptr is possible (panel 12).
            3. 
      
          3. A note must not outlive its house. Don't return or keep the address of a local after its } (panels 19 and 20). The nullptr check can't save you here.
            4. 
      
          4. Notes only look. A plain pointer never decides when a house is knocked down (panel 17).
            5. 
      
          5. Prefer a nickname when the target always exists and never changes: a reference can't be blank and can't be reseated (panel 8).
            6. 
      
          6. Addresses are not data. Never save, print or compare them as answers; they change between runs and machines (panel 2).
            7.

          PART 5 · THE LIBRARY CARDThe stack and the heap, std::unique_ptr, std::move and .get()
          

  
          
    22
    
          A running program keeps its boxes in two parts of town. The stack is a pile of plates: each function call puts a plate on top with its local variables, and the plate is cleared away, every box on it gone, when the call ends. The heap (C++ also calls it the free store) is a library: you ask for a box, it stays out on loan for as long as you like, and it must be handed back when you're done.
          
    
          
      
      
    
          
    
          Why borrow from the heap at all? Three reasons. (1) A box must outlive the block that made it, like a book ordered inside a function and read after it returns. (2) You don't know how many boxes until the program runs. That's why std::vector keeps its items on the heap (Chapter 11's reports said heap-buffer-overflow); the vector borrows and returns for you. (3) Later, in Chapter 24, a box whose exact kind is only decided while running.
          
  
          

  
          
    23
    
          Wren takes us to the library. The librarian, Mabel, runs a tight ship.
          
    
          
      
      
          Mabel says: Every book that leaves my library has exactly one borrower card. Whoever holds the card is responsible for bringing the book back. You can't photocopy a card. You can hand your card to someone else, and then it's their job. And if you drop the card on the way out of the building, my card is enchanted: the book flies home on its own.
          
    
          
    
          
      
          Wren says: That enchanted card is std::unique_ptr<Book>, from the header <memory>. It's a pointer that owns its object: exactly one owner, can't be copied, can be moved, and when the card itself is destroyed, its destructor deletes the book. One book, one card.

              PART 6 · WHY NOT JUST NEW AND DELETE?Raw new and delete, leaks, double frees, and choosing where a box should live
              

  
              
    31
    
              Underneath, make_unique uses the old keywords. new int{42} borrows an int from the heap and gives back a plain pointer to it. delete p; hands it back. Older code and older courses do this by hand, everywhere. The trouble is that a plain pointer is only a note (panel 17): it doesn't know it's supposed to return anything. You must remember, exactly once, on every road out of the function.
              
    
              
      
              FORGET TO RETURNa leak
              The note is gone, the book is still out. Nobody can ever return it. Do it in a loop and the program swells until the machine runs out of memory.
              
      
              RETURN IT TWICEa double free
              The second delete hands back a book that's already on the shelf, or already lent to someone else. Undefined behaviour.
              
    
              
    
              
      
      
              Mabel says: A plain slip of paper with a shelf number on it isn't a borrower card. Nobody's name is on it, and it can't fly the book home. Let me show you what happens to my shelves.

                    What you learned in Chapter 13
                    
    
                      
      
                    • THE NOTE IS NOT THE HOUSEA pointer (int* p) is a box holding an address. &x makes the note; *p follows it to read or write the house; p->m follows it and opens a member. Without a star you change the note (reseat); with a star you change the house.
                      • 
      
                    • CHECK BEFORE YOU FOLLOWnullptr is the blank note. Start every pointer with an address or nullptr, and check if (p) whenever it may be blank. Following nullptr is undefined: load of null pointer, or ASan's SEGV on unknown address 0x000000000000.
                      • 
      
                    • A NOTE NEVER KNOWS THE HOUSE IS GONEA dangling pointer outlives its object (stack-use-after-scope, heap-use-after-free), and a null check can't see it. Never keep the address of a local past its }.
                      • 
      
                    • A PLAIN POINTER ONLY LOOKSPlain pointers observe; they never own or delete. Prefer a reference when the target always exists. Addresses change between runs: never treat them as data.
                      • 
      
                    • ONE BOOK, ONE CARDstd::make_unique<T>(...) borrows from the heap; the std::unique_ptr returns it at its destructor. No copies (use of deleted function); hand over with std::move, after which the old card is empty; lend a look with .get().
                      • 
      
                    • CARDS, NOT SLIPSRaw new/delete leak (detected memory leaks) or free twice (attempting double-free). Choose a plain local first, a vector for many, a unique_ptr for one object that must outlive its block.
                      • 
    
                    
    
                    
      
      
                    Wren says: Notes for looking, cards for owning, and every book goes home exactly once. That's the whole of memory safety in one sentence, and most of C++'s scariest bugs are one of those three rules broken.

    Under the hood: what a pointer really is, what nullptr is, and what unique_ptr costs
    
                    
      
                    A pointer is just a number with a type
                    
      
                    On this machine (64-bit Linux) every pointer is the same size, whatever it points at. sizeof asks how many bytes a type takes:
                    
      sizes.cpp
      
                    
      
                    
      
                     The bits inside are the address. The type (int* versus double*) exists only for the Compiler: it decides how many bytes *p reads and how to interpret them, which is why panel 6's mismatch is refused.
                    
      
                    What "following" costs
                    
      
                    Following a pointer is one extra trip to memory: read the address, then read the house. That's cheap, but not free, which is one reason a plain local or a vector of objects is usually faster than many separate heap objects joined by pointers.
                    
      
                    nullptr and the zero page
                    
      
                    nullptr is a keyword (since C++11) of its own type, std::nullptr_t, that turns into the null value of any pointer type. On common systems that value is the address 0, and the operating system keeps the first page of addresses unmapped, so following a null pointer usually stops the program with a segmentation fault. "Usually" is the catch: the rules call it undefined, and an optimiser may assume it never happens. That's why panels 12 and 13 show only sanitizer runs.
                    
      
                    What g++ does with a returned local address
                    
      
                    Panel 20's program, built with the goggles on, gives a surprise: not a stack report, but a null load. When g++ sees a function return the address of its own local, it warns and then, as its own choice, returns a null pointer instead, so the bug fails fast. Here is the real run:
                    
      
                    
      
                    (Its run command asks UBSan for the call trail, as in panel 12.) Other compilers (and other g++ versions) may do something else; the program is undefined either way.
                    
      
                    AddressSanitizer can also catch a note that outlives its function in general: stack-use-after-return. dangle_uar.cpp keeps the address of make_house's local in a global note, so g++ can't swap in a null. On this machine g++ 13 reports it with no extra options:
                    
      dangle_uar.cpp
      
                    
      
                    
      
                    That check can be switched off for one run with ASAN_OPTIONS=detect_stack_use_after_return=0 ./dangle_uar: then there is no report and the program reads the rubble as if nothing were wrong (checked in a scratch run for this page and not captured: it is undefined behaviour, so this book doesn't show what it prints). Chapter 24's lab finds the same with its own dangling lambda. Clang's AddressSanitizer documentation says the check is switched on by default on Linux. On another system, if a program like this shows no report, run it with =1 instead, which switches the check on.
                    
      
                    unique_ptr costs nothing extra
                    
      
                    A std::unique_ptr<T> with the default deleter holds nothing but the address, so it is the same size as a plain T* (the last line of sizes.cpp above). All the safety is done by the Compiler: the copy constructor is = deleted (panel 27), the move constructor copies the address and sets the source to null, and the destructor calls delete if the pointer isn't null. std::make_unique (C++14) is just a tidy way to write std::unique_ptr<T>(new T(...)) without ever showing new.
                    
      
                    How LeakSanitizer finds leaks
                    
      
                    At exit it pauses the program and scans every place a pointer could live (the stacks, global variables, CPU registers and the heap blocks themselves) for values that look like addresses of live heap blocks. Any block nobody points at is reported as a direct leak; a block reachable only from a leaked block is an indirect leak. It then makes the program exit with a failure code (panel 33's terminal shows exit code 1).
                    
      
                    Two compilers, two wordings
                    
      
                    clang++ 18's version of panel 27's copy error is shorter. Same meaning, different words:
                    
      
                    
      
                    And its version of panel 6's type mismatch: cannot initialize a variable of type 'double *' with an rvalue of type 'int *'. clang++ writes double * with a space; g++ writes double*. Same type.
                    
      
                    
    
                    
  
                    

  
                    
    In real programs
    
                    
      
                      
        
                    • Ownership is the design. In well-written modern C++, owners are almost always plain objects, containers or smart pointers, and plain T* or T& mean "I'm only looking". The C++ Core Guidelines spell this out in rules R.3 (a raw pointer is non-owning), F.7 and F.22 (take T* or T& when you don't take ownership), R.20 and R.21 (use unique_ptr for ownership).
                      • 
        
                    • Factories. Functions that build an object and hand it over (panel 25) often return std::unique_ptr, especially when the caller will later decide what kind of object it is (Chapter 24).
                      • 
        
                    • Trees and linked structures. A tree node that owns its children often holds std::unique_ptr<Node> members: deleting the root deletes everything below it, automatically.
                      • 
        
                    • Not just memory. The same "card returns it" idea works for files, network connections and locks: a class whose destructor gives the thing back. C++ programmers call this RAII ("resource acquisition is initialisation"). unique_ptr can even be given a custom deleter, for example to close a C library's file handle.
                      • 
        
                    • Shared ownership. Sometimes several owners really do need one object and the last one out turns off the lights. That's std::shared_ptr, in Chapter 23, and it costs more.
                      • 
        
                    • Legacy code. Plenty of real code still has raw new and delete, and C libraries hand you pointers you must release with their own functions. Wrapping them in a unique_ptr as early as possible is a common clean-up job.
                      • 
        
                    • Leak checks in testing. Many projects run their tests under AddressSanitizer with LeakSanitizer on, so a new leak turns a test red the day it's written.
                      • 
      
                    
    
                    
  
                    

  
                    
    Common wrong ideas
    
                    
      
                      
        
                    • "A pointer is the object." It's a note with the object's address. Copying the note never copies the house (Boss 1, panel 8's table).
                      • 
        
                    • "p = &b changes what's in a." No star, no house: it rewrites the note. *p = ... changes the house.
                      • 
        
                    • "int* a, b; makes two pointers." It makes one int* (a) and one plain int (b): the star belongs to each name. Declare one pointer per line.
                      • 
        
                    • "If it's not null, it's safe." A dangling pointer isn't null (panel 18).
                      • 
        
                    • "A null dereference always crashes." Usually, not always. It's undefined.
                      • 
        
                    • "I'll compare addresses to see if two values are equal." p == q asks "same house?". *p == *q asks "same contents?".
                      • 
        
                    • "The address I printed is where my variable lives." For that run on that machine. Next run, maybe not (panel 2).
                      • 
        
                    • "Every pointer must be deleted." Only what new made, exactly once, and in modern code you let a unique_ptr do it. Deleting a pointer to a local, or a .get() note, is a disaster.
                      • 
        
                    • "std::move moves the object." It only marks the card as "may be handed over"; the handing over happens in the assignment or call, and the book itself never moves.
                      • 
        
                    • "After a move I can still use the old card." You may check it or give it a new book; following it is a null dereference (panel 30).
                      • 
        
                    • "Smart pointers are slow." unique_ptr costs the same as a raw pointer (Under the hood).
                      • 
        
                    • "Use the heap for big things." Use it when lifetime or count demands it. A vector already puts its items on the heap for you.

    Words from this chapter
    
                    
      
                    
        
                    address
                    The number of the place in memory where a box lives; printed in hexadecimal, different between runs and machines.
                    
        
                    & (address-of)
                    In an expression, &x gives x's address. (Next to a type, int& means a reference.)
                    
        
                    pointer
                    A box that holds an address. int* p is a pointer to an int.
                    
        
                    pointer type
                    What the pointer may point at, such as int* or Robot*. An int's address won't go into a double*.
                    
        
                    dereference (*)
                    Following a pointer to the box it points at: *p reads or writes that box.
                    
        
                    reseat
                    Making a pointer point somewhere else: p = &b;. References can't be reseated.
                    
        
                    nullptr / null pointer
                    The pointer value that points at nothing. Check for it before following a pointer that may hold it.
                    
        
                    -> (arrow)
                    p->m follows the pointer and picks the member m; the same as (*p).m.
                    
        
                    observer
                    A pointer (or reference) that looks at an object but doesn't own it and never deletes it.
                    
        
                    own / owner
                    Being responsible for destroying an object and giving back its memory.
                    
        
                    dangling pointer
                    A pointer to an object that no longer exists.
                    
        
                    uninitialised pointer
                    A pointer created with no value; it holds a garbage address. Using it is undefined.
                    
        
                    segmentation fault (SEGV)
                    The system stopping a program that touched memory it may not use; following a null pointer usually causes one.
                    
        
                    stack
                    The memory for local variables: one plate per function call, cleared when the call (or block) ends.
                    
        
                    heap / free store
                    Memory borrowed on request that stays until it is handed back.
                    
        
                    std::unique_ptr<T>
                    A smart pointer from <memory> that owns exactly one heap object and deletes it in its destructor. Can be moved, not copied.
                    
        
                    smart pointer
                    A class that acts like a pointer and also manages the object's lifetime.
                    
        
                    std::make_unique<T>(...)
                    Builds a T on the heap with the given arguments and returns a unique_ptr<T> owning it.
                    
        
                    std::move
                    Marks an object as one whose contents may be handed over; with a unique_ptr, the card moves and the old one becomes null.
                    
        
                    .get()
                    Gives a plain, non-owning pointer to the object a unique_ptr owns.
                    
        
                    copy constructor
                    The constructor that makes a new object as a copy of an existing one; unique_ptr's is deleted.
                    
        
                    = delete
                    Declares a function only so that any use of it is a compile error.
                    
        
                    factory
                    A function that builds an object and hands it to its caller.
                    
        
                    new / delete
                    The raw keywords to borrow from and give back to the heap. Avoid writing them yourself.
                    
        
                    memory leak
                    Heap memory that is never given back, usually because nothing points at it any more.
                    
        
                    double free
                    Giving back the same heap memory twice; undefined.
                    
        
                    LeakSanitizer (LSan)
                    The part of AddressSanitizer that reports leaks when the program ends.
                    
        
                    stack-use-after-scope
                    ASan's name for using a local variable after its block ended.
                    
        
                    RAII
                    "Resource acquisition is initialisation": a class whose destructor gives back what its constructor took, like unique_ptr.
