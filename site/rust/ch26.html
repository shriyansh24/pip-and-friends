RUST · CHAPTER 26

THE BUSY KITCHEN

Picture: Mabel the librarian waves. Next to her stand three cooks in tall white hats with name badges: Ana holds an order ticket, Ben holds a kitchen knife, and Cy holds a frying pan.

Lunch rush at the library café, and one cook can't keep up. Today Mabel hires a kitchen crew: each cook is a thread, a line of work running at the same time as the others. Cooks need their own ingredients (move), a shift that ends only when everyone is done (join and thread::scope), order tickets on a rail to pass work along (channels), a shared recipe book with a counter of readers (Arc), and one knife they must take turns with (Mutex). On the way: runs that print in a different order every time, two cooks frozen forever, and eight compiler errors and three panics, read like a detective. About 150 minutes, with 30 hands-on checks.

  1. More cooks: spawn, join and who goes first
  2. Give the cook the ingredients: move and scoped threads
  3. Order tickets on a rail: channels
  4. The recipe book and the one knife: Arc and Mutex
  5. Kitchen trouble: poisoning, deadlock, RwLock, atomics, Send and Sync
  6. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · MORE COOKSa thread is a cook; thread::spawn hires one and join waits for them; output order is up to the operating system; forgetting to join, getting a result back, and a cook who panics

1

Noon at the library café. Forty lunch orders, one stove, one cook: Mabel. Every program in this book so far has worked like that: one line of work, doing one step after another, from the top of main to the bottom.

Mabel says: While the soup simmers, I just stand there waiting. The salad could be getting made! I need more cooks working at the same time.

The Professor says: In a program, a cook is called a thread. Your main function already runs on one: the main thread. Rust lets you start more. Fair warning: a kitchen with several cooks needs rules, and Rust checks most of them before anyone lights a stove.

Picture: Mabel, worried, shrugs at a pile of order tickets. The Professor answers from the right.

2

A thread is one line of work inside a running program. All the threads of a program share the same kitchen (its memory), and each has its own little worktop (its own stack, Chapter 7). Running several at once is called concurrency.

Picture: three cooks side by side. The first wears a MAIN badge and holds an order ticket; the other two, Ben and Cy, each hold a frying pan.

Mabel says: Who decides which cook works at each moment? Not me, and not you. The operating system (Windows, macOS, Linux) is the shift manager. It hands out turns on the computer's cores, and it doesn't promise any order.

    scope_race.rs
    

    

    
    
      
  1. error[E0499]: cannot borrow `plates` as mutable more than once at a time: Chapter 8's "one writer" rule. The labels show first mutable borrow occurs here (line 6) and second mutable borrow occurs here (line 7).
    2. 
      
  2. Why this matters: plates += 1 is really three steps: read the number, add one, write it back. Two threads doing that at the same instant can both read 0 and both write 1. One plate disappears. Two threads touching the same memory at once, at least one of them writing, with nothing to keep them in turn, is a data race. In many languages it compiles and gives wrong answers now and then.
    3. 
      
  3. This is also why Chapter 25's set_var needs unsafe. The environment is one shared table for the whole program, and outside Windows the operating system offers no thread-safe way to read it, so a thread changing it while another reads it is a data race that the Compiler can't see.

Real kitchens don't shout across the room. They clip order tickets onto a rail, and the cook at the end takes them off in the order they arrived. Rust's version is a channel: one end to send with, one end to receive with.

PART 4 · THE RECIPE BOOK AND THE ONE KNIFERc can't cross threads (E0277); Arc shares one value with a thread-safe counter, read-only (E0596); Mutex gives one thread at a time the right to change it; the lock guard; a Mutex moved in a loop (E0382); Arc<Mutex<T>>

    rc_thread.rs
    

    

    
    
      
  1. error[E0277]: `Rc<Vec<&str>>` cannot be sent between threads safely: what, in plain words. The long underline covers the closure, lines 7 to 9, which carries the Rc into the thread.
    2. 
      
  2. = help: … the trait `Send` is not implemented for `Rc<Vec<&str>>`. Send is a badge (Chapter 17) for types that are safe to hand to another thread. spawn demands it: note: required by a bound in `spawn`.
    3. 
      
  3. Why no badge? Rc's counter is changed with plain arithmetic. Two threads cloning at the same instant could both read 2 and both write 3: the same lost update as panel 15. A wrong count means the value is freed too early, or never.
    4. 
    

  


  

    24
    
Arc<T> is the thread-safe twin: Atomically Reference Counted. Picture one recipe book on a stand with a clicker counter. Every cook who wants to read it clicks in; when they're done they click out. When the counter reaches 0, the book is put away. The clicker is atomic: each click happens as one step that can't be cut in half, even if two cooks click at the same instant.

    

    

      
      
Mabel says: Same idea as Rc: Arc::clone clicks the counter, it doesn't copy the book. Atomic clicks cost a tiny bit more than plain ones, which is why Rust gives you both and lets you pick.

The kitchen has one good knife, and the rule is: nobody touches the chopping board without holding it. That's a Mutex<T> (mutual exclusion): a lock wrapped around a value, so only one thread at a time can get at it.

    

    
    
      
  1. Lock: m.lock() asks for the knife. If it's on the hook, you get it at once.
    2. 
      
  2. Wait: if another thread holds it, lock() blocks: your thread stops and waits until the knife comes back.
    3. 
      
  3. Hold: what you get back is a MutexGuard, the knife in your hand. It derefs to the value inside (Chapter 23), so you can read and change it.
    4. 
      
  4. Put back: when the guard is dropped, usually at a closing brace, the lock opens by itself. You can't forget to unlock: that's Drop doing its job (RAII, Chapter 23).
    5.

One thread first, to watch the knife. try_lock() is the polite version of lock(): it never waits, it just answers whether the knife is free right now.

    A Mutex lets only one thread in, even if all of them just want to read. For data that's read often and changed rarely, like the day's menu, there's RwLock (a reader-writer lock): read() lets many readers in at once, write() lets in one writer and nobody else.
    
    rwlock.rs
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    1. Lines 5–6: two read guards at the same time: 1 and 1. A Mutex would have blocked the second.
      2. 
      
    2. Line 8: while readers are in, try_write() fails: false. (A plain write() would never get in here: this thread itself holds the read locks, so it would wait forever, or panic, which the standard library's docs allow.)
      3. 
      
    3. Lines 9–11: readers leave, and the writer gets in. menu.write().unwrap().push("pie") holds the write guard only until the semicolon.
      4. 
    
    
    
    
      
      
    Mabel says: That's my oldest rule, now guarding threads: lend it to many readers, or to one writer, never both at once. The Compiler checks it for & and &mut; RwLock checks it while the program runs.

    For a plain number that many threads add to, a whole lock is more than you need. Atomic types, like AtomicUsize, are numbers whose operations happen in one uncuttable step, like the clicker on the recipe book.
    
    atomic.rs
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    1. Line 5: AtomicUsize::new(0), not mut: atomics, like Mutex, change through a shared reference.
      2. 
      
    2. Line 10: fetch_add(1, …) reads, adds and writes as one step, so no update is lost: always 4000. Scoped threads borrow plates directly, so no Arc is needed here.
      3. 
      
    3. Ordering::Relaxed says how this operation lines up with other memory operations. For a counter that nothing else depends on, Relaxed is enough. The other orderings are an advanced topic beyond this book (see the notebook's go-deeper page).
      4. 
    
    
  
    

  
    
  

  
    
    37
    
    How does the Compiler know Rc can't cross threads but Arc can? Two marker traits: badges with no methods, which a type wears or doesn't (Chapter 17's badges). Rust pins them on automatically: a type gets a badge when everything inside it has that badge.
    
    
    
      
    Send"can be handed to another thread"
    Moving it into a thread is safe. Almost everything: i32, String, Vec<T>, Arc<T>, Mutex<T>. Not: Rc<T> (its counter isn't atomic) and a MutexGuard (a lock must be opened by the thread that closed it).
    
      
    Sync"can be shared between threads"
    Several threads may hold & to it at once. T is Sync exactly when &T is Send. Yes: i32, Mutex<T>, RwLock<T>, atomics. Not: Cell and RefCell, whose unguarded changes through & would race.
    
    
    
    sync_error.rs
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    1. error[E0277]: `Cell<i32>` cannot be shared between threads safely. This time it's Sync that's missing: the scoped thread only borrows count, and note: required for `&Cell<i32>` to implement `Send` connects the two badges.
      2. 
      
    2. The note: even names the fixes: std::sync::RwLock or std::sync::atomic::AtomicI32, the thread-safe versions of this whiteboard.
      3. 
    
    
    
    
      
      
    The Compiler says: I check the badges every time a value crosses into a thread. That's how a data race becomes a compile error instead of a bug that shows up on a Tuesday. Rust's own name for this is fearless concurrency.

    What you learned in Chapter 26
    
    
      
      
    • EACH COOK KEEPS THEIR OWN ORDER; NOBODY PROMISES THE MIXA thread is a line of work. thread::spawn starts one, join waits and returns its result (Err if it panicked). Output from several threads can mix differently on every run: predict what could happen, and let main print joined results for steady output.
      • 
      
    • DON'T LOCK UP UNTIL EVERY COOK HAS CLOCKED OUTWhen main ends, every thread stops. Keep the handles and join them, or use thread::scope, which joins for you at its brace.
      • 
      
    • A COOK MIGHT OUTLIVE YOU, SO GIVE, DON'T LENDSpawned threads can't borrow locals (E0373): use move, and the value is gone from main (E0382). Scoped threads may borrow. Two writers at once is a data race, stopped by the Compiler (E0499).
      • 
      
    • CLIP IT ON, LET IT GOChannels: tx.send moves the value (E0382 after). Clone senders for more producers; the receiver's loop ends when every sender is dropped (forget one and it waits forever). sync_channel(n) is a short rail.
      • 
      
    • ARC TO SHARE, MUTEX TO CHANGERc isn't Send (E0277), Arc is, and it's read-only (E0596). Mutex: lock() gives a guard that unlocks when dropped. Clone the Arc per thread (or E0382 "in previous iteration of loop"). A panic while locked poisons the Mutex.
      • 
      
    • ALWAYS ASK FOR THE TOOLS IN THE SAME ORDERDeadlock: two threads each hold what the other needs. It compiles; agree on one lock order. RwLock: many readers or one writer. Atomics: lock-free counters. Send and Sync are the badges behind it all.

    Under the hood: what a thread, a lock and an atomic really are
    
    
      
    
        
        
    The Professor says: Rust's std::thread threads are the operating system's own threads. Rust adds no scheduler of its own, only rules about what may cross between them.
    
      
    
      
    Threads and cores
    
      
    Each thread::spawn asks the operating system for a new thread with its own stack (2 MiB by default for spawned threads on most platforms; thread::Builder::stack_size changes it). The OS scheduler gives threads turns on the CPU cores, switching many times a second. On a 2-core machine, two threads can truly run at the same instant (parallelism); with more threads than cores they take turns (concurrency). That's why the order in panel 4 changes: it depends on what else your computer is doing.
    
      
    Why 'static and Send
    
      
    spawn's signature is roughly fn spawn<F, T>(f: F) -> JoinHandle<T> where F: FnOnce() -> T + Send + 'static, T: Send + 'static. 'static rules out borrowing locals (E0373); Send rules out Rc and friends (E0277). thread::scope's spawn only needs the closure to live as long as the scope, which is why it can borrow.
    
      
    Mutex
    
      
    On Linux, today's std::sync::Mutex is a small integer in memory plus the kernel's futex system call: an uncontended lock is one atomic instruction, and only a thread that must wait asks the kernel to put it to sleep. The lock isn't re-entrant: locking it again on the thread that already holds it is never allowed to succeed, and it may deadlock or panic (the docs leave it unspecified). Poisoning is a flag set when a guard is dropped during a panic.
    
      
    Atomics and orderings
    
      
    An atomic operation like fetch_add compiles to a special CPU instruction (on x86-64, lock xadd) that no other core can interrupt halfway. Ordering controls something subtler: whether other memory writes made around it are seen by other threads in the same order. Relaxed promises only that the counter itself is never torn or lost; Acquire/Release and SeqCst add ordering guarantees needed when an atomic flag protects other data. Mara Bos's free book Rust Atomics and Locks explains all of it.
    
      
    Channels
    
      
    Since Rust 1.67, std::sync::mpsc is built on the same design as the crossbeam-channel crate. channel() is unbounded (it grows as needed); sync_channel(n) is bounded, and sync_channel(0) is a rendezvous channel. The standard library also has an unstable mpmc (many receivers) module, usable only on nightly Rust; for many receivers, the crossbeam-channel crate is the usual choice.

    In real programs: where threads turn up
    
    
      
      
        
    • Worker pools: servers keep a fixed number of threads and hand them jobs through a channel. Chapter 29 builds one, as the Rust book's final project does.
      • 
        
    • Rayon: the rayon crate turns .iter() into .par_iter() and splits the work across all cores, using the same Send and Sync rules to stay safe.
      • 
        
    • Shared configuration: Arc<Config> handed to every worker, read-only, is one of the most common patterns in Rust services; Arc<RwLock<Cache>> for data that changes now and then.
      • 
        
    • Background work in desktop and game programs: a thread loads files or computes while the main thread keeps the window responsive, and sends results back over a channel.
      • 
        
    • Atomics count requests, hand out unique IDs (AtomicU64::fetch_add), and hold "please stop" flags (AtomicBool) that a long-running thread checks.
      • 
        
    • Lots of waiting, little computing (thousands of network connections): there, one thread per connection gets expensive, and programs use async instead. That's Chapter 27.
      • 
        
    • parking_lot and crossbeam are popular crates with alternative Mutex, RwLock and channel types.

    Common wrong ideas
    
    
      
      
        
    • "Threads run in the order I spawn them." Usually close, never promised (panel 4's table).
      • 
        
    • "A sleep makes the order safe." Sleeping only makes a particular order likely. Use join, a channel or a lock when the order matters.
      • 
        
    • "Spawned threads finish before the program exits." Only if someone joins them (panel 6).
      • 
        
    • "move copies the values." It moves them. Only Copy types like i32 leave a copy behind.
      • 
        
    • "Arc makes the value changeable from every thread." Arc only shares. Changing needs a Mutex, an RwLock or an atomic inside.
      • 
        
    • "Safe Rust can't deadlock." It prevents data races, not deadlocks (or leaks, or logic bugs).
      • 
        
    • "I must remember to unlock." The guard unlocks when dropped. The real risk is holding it too long: keep locked sections short, and don't call lock() on the same Mutex again while you hold it.
      • 
        
    • "More threads is always faster." Beyond the number of cores, threads take turns, and every lock they share makes them wait for each other.

    Words from this chapter
    
    
      
    
        
    thread
    One line of work inside a running program; all of a program's threads share its memory.
    
        
    main thread
    The thread main runs on. When it ends, the program ends.
    
        
    concurrency / parallelism
    Several things in progress at once (taking turns or not) / truly running at the same instant on different cores.
    
        
    scheduler
    The part of the operating system that decides which thread runs when.
    
        
    thread::spawn / JoinHandle
    Starts a thread running a closure / the claim ticket it returns.
    
        
    join
    Wait for a thread to finish; returns Ok(its result) or Err if it panicked.
    
        
    move closure
    A closure that takes ownership of the values it uses.
    
        
    scoped thread / thread::scope
    A thread that must finish before the scope's closing brace, so it may borrow locals.
    
        
    interleaving
    One particular way several threads' steps got mixed in a run.
    
        
    data race
    Two threads touching the same memory at the same time, at least one writing, with nothing to order them. Safe Rust forbids it.
    
        
    channel / mpsc
    A one-way queue between threads: senders (tx) and one receiver (rx); "multiple producer, single consumer".
    
        
    bounded channel
    sync_channel(n): room for n messages; a full one makes send wait.
    
        
    Arc<T>
    Atomically reference-counted pointer: several owners on several threads, read-only.
    
        
    atomic
    Done as one uncuttable step that no other thread can see half-finished.
    
        
    Mutex<T>
    Mutual exclusion: a lock around a value, so one thread at a time can use it.
    
        
    lock guard / MutexGuard
    What lock() returns; gives access to the value, and unlocks when dropped.
    
        
    block
    When a thread stops and waits (for a lock, a message or a join).
    
        
    poisoning
    The mark a Mutex gets when a thread panics while holding it; lock() then returns Err(PoisonError).
    
        
    deadlock
    Threads waiting for each other in a circle, forever.
    
        
    Barrier
    A gate that opens only when a set number of threads have reached it.
    
        
    RwLock<T>
    A lock that lets in many readers or one writer.
    
        
    Send / Sync
    Marker traits: safe to hand to another thread / safe to share by & between threads.
    
        
    E0373 / E0499 / E0382
    Closure may outlive the current function (it borrows); two mutable borrows at once; use of a moved value (including "in previous iteration of loop").
    
        
    E0277 / E0596
    A missing trait, here Send or Sync ("cannot be sent / shared between threads safely"); cannot borrow data in an Arc as mutable.
