BORROW&book
Putting & in front of a value makes a reference: a note that says "the book is over there". Handing out a reference is called borrowing.

      
READ-ONLY&String
The type of that note is &String, said "reference to a String" or "ref String". A plain & reference only lets you look. It is also called a shared reference.

    To write notes in the margins, you can't sit in the reading room. You check the book out, and take it to a study booth, alone.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    WRITE&mut book
    &mut makes a mutable reference: a pass that lets you change the value. You still don't own it, so you can't give it away or drop it.
    
      
    ALONEexclusive
    While a writing loan is open, nobody else may use the book at all: no other writers, no readers, not even the owner. That's why it's also called an exclusive (or unique) reference.

      PART 3 · THE LEDGER RULEMany readers or one writer: why, and the errors E0499 and E0502
      

  
      
    18
    
    
      Mabel's ledger has one rule. At any moment, a book may be lent to…
      
    
      
      
      MANY READERS&  &  &
      Any number of shared references at once. ALLOWED
      
      
      ONE WRITER&mut
      Exactly one mutable reference, and nobody else at all. ALLOWED
      
      
      BOTH AT ONCE& + &mut
      Readers and a writer together, or two writers. NEVER
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
      Mabel says: Lend it to many readers, or to one writer. Never both at once. Catchphrase: many readers or one writer.
      
    
      
    
      Three cards: many readers, allowed; one writer, allowed; both at once, never. Mabel points at her ledger.
      
  
      

  
      
    19
    
      Why so strict? Here's what could go wrong. A reader writes down where shelf slot 0 is. Then someone adds a book, the shelf is full, and the whole shelf moves to a bigger room.
      
    
    
    
      
      
      
      Mabel says: The reader's note now points at an empty, freed room. That's a dangling reference, and in some languages reading it gives garbage, or a crash, or a security hole. Rust's answer: while anyone is reading, nobody may write. Then this can never happen.

      Draw each loan as a bar, from the line where it's made to the line where it's last used. Not to the closing brace. Bars of a writer must never overlap any other bar.
      
    
    
    
      Readers are solid sky bars, the writer is a dashed yellow bar. The ✗ marks the overlap.
      
  
      

  
      
    24
    
      The top timeline, as a program.
      
    last_use.rs
    
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
      Mabel says: r1 and r2 still exist until the closing brace, but they're never used after line 5. So I cross their loans out of my ledger right there, and the writer on line 6 is welcome. Catchphrase: a loan ends at its last use.
      
    
      
    
      The feature's official name is non-lexical lifetimes: a loan's life isn't tied to the braces (the "lexical" layout of the code) but to how the reference is actually used. Older books and blog posts, written before the borrow checker learned this, may show programs like this one failing.

Lending takes some away from the owner; the loan's end gives them back.

          PART 5 · BRING IT BACK BEFORE THE OWNER LEAVESReferences never dangle: E0597
          

  
          
    28
    
          Inside a block, an owner leaves at the closing brace, and their book is cleared away (it's dropped, Chapter 7). What if someone outside the block is still holding a card that points at it?
          
    
    
    
          
      
      
          Mabel says: A card pointing at a book that's gone is a dangling reference again. I never issue one. Catchphrase: bring it back before the owner leaves. In plain words: a reference can never outlive its owner.

          &xLend x for reading (a shared reference).As many as you like at once. The owner keeps R.

          What you learned in Chapter 8
          
    
            
      
          • LOOK, DON'T TAKE&x lends x for reading; the type is &T. Functions that only look take &String, and the caller keeps its value. A reference owns nothing, so dropping it frees nothing.
            • 
      
          • SAY MUT THREE TIMESTo write through a reference: let mut x, lend &mut x, accept &mut T. Missing one gives E0596. That's the whole secret of read_line(&mut guess).
            • 
      
          • THE STAR FOLLOWS THE ARROW*r is the value r points at: *r += 1. Methods (r.len()) and println! follow the arrow for you.
            • 
      
          • MANY READERS OR ONE WRITERAny number of &, or exactly one &mut and nothing else. Two writers: E0499. A reader and a writer: E0502.
            • 
      
          • A LOAN ENDS AT ITS LAST USENot at the closing brace. Read the "later used here" label: that late use is often the culprit.
            • 
      
          • BRING IT BACK BEFORE THE OWNER LEAVESA reference can never outlive its owner: E0597. Read, write and own permissions come and go with every loan.
            •

    Under the hood: what a reference is made of, and what the borrow checker costs
    
          
      
          
        
        
          The Professor says: A reference is about the simplest thing a computer has: a memory address. Everything clever about it happens before the program runs.
          
      
          
      
          A reference is an address
          
      
          In memory, &book is just the address of book's slot on the stack: a number saying where to look. On a 64-bit computer that's 8 bytes, whatever it points at. A reference to a 24-byte String is 8 bytes; a reference to an array of a thousand numbers is still 8 bytes. That's why lending is cheap and why functions take & for big things.
          
      sizes.rs
          
      
          (std::mem::size_of::<T>() asks how many bytes a type takes. These sizes are for this 64-bit Linux machine; a 32-bit system would show 4 for each reference.)
          
      
          The capacities in panel 11, measured
          
      
          Panel 11 draws book's room growing from 4 bytes to 20. This program makes the same String the same way and asks it for its capacity() before and after the note.
          
      caps.rs
          
      
          Permissions don't exist at run time
          
      
          R, W and O live only in the Compiler's head. The program that finally runs contains no ledger, no counters and no checks for borrowing: & and &mut become the same kind of plain address. The borrow checker is the part of the Compiler that does the bookkeeping, and when it is satisfied it leaves nothing behind. Safety at zero run-time cost is the whole point.
          
      
          Why &mut must be alone
          
      
          Two names that lead to the same data are called aliases. Aliases alone are harmless (many readers), and changes alone are harmless (one writer). Aliases plus changes are what cause trouble: a reader's note pointing into a buffer that a writer just reallocated, as in panel 19. Rust's rule, "aliasing XOR mutation", rules that out entirely. The Brown edition of the Rust book calls it the Pointer Safety Principle.
          
      
          The dot and auto-deref
          
      
          When you write r.len(), the Compiler sees that len belongs to String, not to &String, so it inserts the * for you: (*r).len(). It does the same through several layers (a reference to a reference). It also works the other way: book.len() really calls String::len(&book), lending book for the call. That's why shelf.push(40) in panel 21 counted as a writing loan: push takes &mut self, so the dot quietly lends &mut shelf. Methods and self are Chapter 10.
          
      
          Two loans of two different things
          
      
          In Boss 2, read(&mut total, &mut best, pages) has two &mut at once and compiles fine, because the rule counts loans per value. The borrow checker can also tell apart different fields of a struct or tuple, so &mut pair.0 and &mut pair.1 can coexist. It can't tell apart v[0] and v[1] of a vector, because an index is only known at run time; Chapter 9 shows the tool for that.
          
    
          
  
          

  
          
    In real programs: how professionals use & and &mut
    
          
      
            
        
          • Take & by default. Most functions only need to look at their inputs. Asking for a reference keeps the caller free to use its value afterwards, and costs 8 bytes instead of a copy.
            • 
        
          • Use &mut to update in place. Filling a buffer (read_line), adding to a list, adjusting a score: the caller can see from &mut x at the call that x may change.
            • 
        
          • Take ownership only when you keep it. A function that stores the value somewhere for later, or turns it into something else, takes it by value (a move).
            • 
        
          • Looping without giving the list away. for x in &list reads each item; for x in &mut list lets you change each item through *x. Remember Chapter 7's for x in v, which moved the whole vector into the loop.
            • 
      
          
      in_real.rs
          
      
            
        
          • A small preview: experienced Rust programmers write &str instead of &String, and &[i32] instead of &Vec<i32>, for parameters. Those are slices, coming in Chapter 9, and they accept more kinds of input. &String is perfectly correct; it's just less flexible.
            • 
        
          • Threads (Chapter 26) rely on these same rules: two threads writing the same data at once is a data race, and in safe Rust it won't compile, for exactly the reason two &mut won't.

    Common wrong ideas
    
          
      
            
        
          • "&x makes a copy of x." It makes a small address that points at x. Nothing is copied, nothing is moved.
            • 
        
          • "A reference owns the value too, a little bit." It owns nothing. When a reference goes away, nothing is freed. Only the owner drops the value.
            • 
        
          • "&mut means the reference itself can be changed to point elsewhere." It means you can change the value it points at. Re-pointing a reference variable needs let mut r, which is a different thing.
            • 
        
          • "let r = &mut x; works whenever r is mut." The owner x must be let mut. You can't lend a right to write that you don't have (E0596).
            • 
        
          • "A loan lasts until the closing brace." It lasts until the reference's last use (non-lexical lifetimes).
            • 
        
          • "Two &mut in one line is always an error." Only two loans of the same value. Lending &mut a and &mut b together is fine.
            • 
        
          • "The error is on the line with the caret." The caret marks where the conflict was noticed. The cause is often the "later used here" line or the signature in the help:.
            • 
        
          • "Printing a reference prints an address." println!("{r}") follows the arrow and prints the value.
            • 
        
          • "* is for multiplying only." Between two values it multiplies (a * b); in front of a reference it follows the arrow (*r). Line 7 of deref.rs uses both: *r = *r * 10.
            • 
        
          • "The borrow checker slows my program down." All of its work happens while compiling. At run time a reference is a plain address.

    Words from this chapter
    
          
      
          
        
          reference
          A value that points at another value without owning it. Made with & or &mut.
          
        
          borrow / borrowing
          Making a reference: lending a value without giving it away.
          
        
          shared reference, &T
          A read-only reference. Any number can exist at once. Also called an immutable reference.
          
        
          mutable reference, &mut T
          A reference you can change the value through. Only one at a time, and nothing else meanwhile. Also called an exclusive or unique reference.
          
        
          loan
          Mabel's word for one borrow, from when it's made to its last use.
          
        
          dereference, *
          Follow a reference to the value it points at: *r.
          
        
          auto-deref
          The dot (r.len()) and println! follow references for you.
          
        
          borrow checker
          The part of the Compiler that checks every loan against the rules, before the program runs.
          
        
          non-lexical lifetimes
          The rule that a loan ends at the reference's last use, not at the closing brace.
          
        
          place
          Anything that can hold a value and be borrowed: a variable, *r, an element v[0], a field.
          
        
          permissions (R, W, O)
          Read, Write, Own: what the borrow checker lets you do with a place at each line. A teaching model from the Brown edition of the Rust book.
          
        
          alias
          A second name or path to the same data.
          
        
          dangling reference
          A reference to memory that has been freed. Safe Rust never lets one exist.
          
        
          reallocate
          Move a String's or Vec's contents to a bigger heap block when they outgrow the old one.
          
        
          E0499 / E0502 / E0596 / E0597
          Two writers; a reader and a writer; writing without permission; a reference that outlives its owner. Bonus: E0505, moving a value while it's lent.
          
        
          Vec, vec![...]
          A growable list. This chapter draws it as a shelf; Chapter 11, which covers it in depth, calls it the accordion folder.
