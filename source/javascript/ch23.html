Every JavaScript number is stored in 64 bits: 64 tiny on/off lights, each 0 or 1. They're split into three groups, the same way scientists write very big and very small numbers.

    

    

      
The Professor says: A number is stored as ± 1.something × 2power. The sign says plus or minus. The exponent says how far to slide the binary point. The fraction holds the digits. This layout has a long name, IEEE 754 double precision, or "a 64-bit float" for short. "Float" because the point floats left and right.

Here's the big consequence. The fraction always has 52 bits. So between 1 and 2 there are exactly as many marks as between 1024 and 2048, or between a trillion and two trillion. The further you walk along the ruler, the further apart the marks.

    

    
    gaps.js
    

    

    
    
      
  1. gapNear works out the distance from x to the next mark up: 2 to the power (x's exponent − 52). The second line of each pair tests it: adding the gap moves to the next mark (true), adding a quarter of the gap rounds straight back (false).
    2. 
      
  2. Near a quadrillion (1e15) the marks are an eighth apart. Near 253 they are 2 apart: whole numbers start to fall between marks.
    3. 
    

    
THE MARKS SPREAD OUT

CLOSE ENOUGH, NOT EXACTLY

  


  

  

  

    9
    
Whole numbers sit exactly on marks… until the gap grows past 1. That happens at 253. Every whole number up to Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253 − 1) is safe: it has its own mark, and so does its neighbour.

    safe.js
    

    

    
    
      
  1. Lines 3 and 4: 2 ** 53 + 1 has no mark, so it rounds back to 253. Line 5 proves the two are equal.
    2. 
      
  2. Number.isSafeInteger asks: is it a whole number, and is it in the zone where every whole number has a mark?
    3. 
      
  3. Number.isInteger(5.0) is true: 5 and 5.0 are the very same number in JavaScript. "5" is a string, so false. And 1e300 is a whole number (huge numbers have no fraction part left), but nowhere near safe.
    4. 
    

    

      
      
The Professor says: Where does this bite? IDs. A database may hand out 64-bit ID numbers such as 1234567890123456789. Read into a plain number, the last digits get rounded and two different IDs can become equal. Keep such IDs as strings, or use the abacus in Part 3.

Remember the sign bit? It has a funny side effect. Flip it on zero and you get minus zero, -0: a real, separate value.

    negzero.js

    2. 
      
  2. Dividing by it gives -Infinity: the sign still matters there.
    3. 
      
  3. Turning it into text with String, a template or JSON drops the minus: 0. And rounding a small negative number, or multiplying a negative by 0, makes one.
    4. 
    

    

      
      
Kit says: So if a temperature display ever says "-0 °C", now you know: something rounded a tiny negative number and printed it with console.log. Show it with a template string, or add 0 (-0 + 0 is 0).

    

  


  

  

  
  
PART 2 · ROUNDING AND READING NUMBERStoFixed, toPrecision, parseInt and its radix, separators


  

    11
    
Chapter 3 met toFixed: a number in, text out, with a fixed number of decimal places. Here are its surprises in one place, plus its cousin toPrecision, which counts significant digits (digits from the first non-zero one) instead of decimal places.

    tofixed.js
    

    

    
    
      
  1. Line 1: 2.5 sits exactly on a mark, halfway between 2 and 3. toFixed breaks ties by going away from zero: 3.
    2. 
      
  2. Lines 2 and 3: 1.005 is stored a hair below 1.005 (20 places show it), so there is no tie at all. It rounds down: 1.00.
    3. 
      
  3. Line 4: for -2.5, toFixed goes away from zero (-3) but Math.round goes up, towards plus infinity (-2). Two rounding rules!
    4. 
      
  4. Line 6: toPrecision(2) on 123456 can't fit it in two digits without an exponent, so it writes 1.2e+5.
    5. 
      
  5. Line 7: to get a number back, wrap the text in Number(…). 19.999 rounds to "20.00", which is the number 20.
    6. 
      
  6. Line 8: the same hair hides in a multiplication. 1.005 * 100 prints 100.49999999999999, just below 100.5, so rounding to cents by hand with Math.round gives 1, not 1.01.
    7. 
    

  


  

    12
    
Three tools, three jobs. Pick by what you need to hand on.

    

      
Math.round(x)
Gives a number, a whole one. Ties go up (towards +∞), so -2.5 becomes -2.
For: counting, array indexes, whole steps.

      
x.toFixed(d)
Gives text with exactly d decimal places. Ties go away from zero. Rounds the value really stored.
For: showing a value to people.

      
x.toPrecision(p)
Gives text with p significant digits, switching to e notation when needed.
For: science-style output, "about 1.2 × 10⁵".

    

    

      
      
Kit says: Round only at the very end, when the number is about to be shown. Round in the middle of a calculation and the little errors pile up.

    PART 6 · THE TOOLBOX: TEMPORALPlainDate, PlainTime, ZonedDateTime and Duration
    

  
    
    38
    
    Date is one tool trying to do every job: a pin on a tape and a calendar and a clock, always tied to UTC or the machine's zone, and changeable in place. Temporal is JavaScript's new answer: a toolbox with a separate tool for each job.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    PlainDate: a calendar date, no time, no zone. A birthday, a due date, opening night.
    
      
    PlainTime: a wall-clock time, no date. "Curtain up at 19:30 every night."
    
      
    ZonedDateTime: an exact moment in a named place. A live stream at 19:30 in Chicago.
    
      
    Duration: a length of time. "2 hours 15 minutes", "3 months".
    
      
    Instant: a pin on the tape and nothing else, like a Date's mark. Log timestamps.
    
      
    Temporal.Now: asks the computer's clock, giving any of these for "right now".

    What you learned in Chapter 23
    
    
      
      
    • THE MARKS SPREAD OUTEvery number is 64 bits: sign, exponent, fraction, ± 1.fraction × 2power. With 52 fraction bits, marks get further apart as numbers grow; past Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253 − 1) even whole numbers fall between them. Number.isInteger, Number.isSafeInteger. -0 exists: Object.is sees it, === doesn't.
      • 
      
    • CLOSE ENOUGH, NOT EXACTLYNever compare decimals with ===. Use Math.abs(a - b) < Number.EPSILON, scaled for big numbers, or count whole steps. toFixed gives text and rounds the stored value ((1.005).toFixed(2) is "1.00"); Math.round sends ties up. parseInt reads until it can't: always give a radix, never give it a number.
      • 
      
    • THE ABACUS HAS NO DECIMAL POINTBigInt (10n) keeps every digit of any whole number. Division truncates. Three walls: no mixing (Cannot mix BigInt and other types), no Math, no JSON without a replacer. BigInt(1.5) is a RangeError.
      • 
      
    • CENTS IN, FORMAT OUTStore money as whole cents; show it with Intl.NumberFormat (currency, percent, compact). With no locale, output follows the machine; name the locale for files and tests.
      • 
      
    • ONE TAPE, MANY CLOCKSA Date is one mark: milliseconds since 1970 UTC. Months count from 0 and values roll over. A date-only string is UTC midnight; getDate() reads on the machine's clock. Invalid dates are silent until RangeError: Invalid time value. Setters change a shared Date in place: copy first.
      • 
      
    • ONE TOOL PER JOBTemporal: PlainDate, PlainTime, ZonedDateTime, Instant, Duration. Immutable, months from 1, add({ months: 1 }) clamps, impossible dates are refused. Node 26+; feature-detect elsewhere.

    Under the hood: the 64 bits, rounding rules and what a Date really holds
    
    
      
    Decoding the bits exactly
    
      
    For a normal number, the value is (−1)sign × 1.fraction (binary) × 2exponent − 1023. The 1023 is called the bias: it lets 11 unsigned bits stand for powers from −1022 to +1023. Two exponent patterns are special. All zeros means zero or a subnormal number (no hidden 1, used for the tiniest values, down to Number.MIN_VALUE, about 5 × 10−324); that's also where the two zeros come from, sign 0 or sign 1. All ones means Infinity (fraction all zero) or NaN (anything else).
    
      
    Why 0.1's last bit ends in …1010
    
      
    In binary, 1/10 is 0.000110011001100… repeating. Rounding to 52 fraction bits uses round half to even: the result is the nearer of the two neighbouring marks, and in a tie the one whose last bit is 0. That's why xray.js shows 0.1's fraction ending in 1010 instead of 1001: the bits after the cut were more than half, so it rounded up. Every arithmetic result is rounded the same way, once per operation.
    
      
    What console.log prints
    
      
    A stored number is an exact binary value with up to 767 significant decimal digits. console.log and String print the shortest decimal that would round back to the same mark. That's why 0.1 prints as 0.1 even though the mark is 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827…, and why the sum 0.1 + 0.2 needs 17 digits to be told apart from 0.3's mark.
    
      
    Number.EPSILON precisely
    
      
    It is 2−52, the gap between 1 and the next mark up. Near a value x the gap is about x × EPSILON, which is why a fixed EPSILON is too strict for big numbers and far too loose for tiny ones (comparing 1e-20 with 2e-20 using a plain EPSILON calls them equal). gaps.js computes the gap as 2⌊log₂ x⌋ − 52; this is exact for normal numbers in practice, and the program tests it rather than trusting it.
    
      
    The two rounding rules
    
      
    Math.round(x) is defined as the integer closest to x, with ties going towards +∞. Number.prototype.toFixed picks the integer n for which n / 10f − x is closest to zero, and if there are two, the larger n, after taking the sign off. Both work on the exact stored value, never on the decimal you typed.
    
      
    What parseInt really does
    
      
    It converts its argument to a string, trims leading white space, reads an optional sign, then reads the longest run of digits valid in the radix. If no radix is given (or it's 0), it uses 16 for a 0x/0X start and 10 otherwise. A radix outside 2 to 36 gives NaN. Since ES5, a leading 0 no longer means octal.
    
      
    BigInt inside
    
      
    A BigInt is stored as a sign plus as many 64-bit "digits" as needed, so it can grow until memory runs out (V8 has an upper size limit of about a billion bits, far beyond any normal use). Its operations take longer the more digits it has. BigInt.asIntN(64, x) and BigInt.asUintN(64, x) wrap a value into 64 bits, which is how code talks to databases and binary formats.
    
      
    What a Date holds
    
      
    A Date object has exactly one internal value, called [[DateValue]]: a number of milliseconds since the epoch, or NaN for an invalid date. Its range is ±8.64 × 1015 ms, 100 million days either side of 1970 (years −271821 to 275760). It ignores leap seconds: every day has exactly 86 400 000 ms on its tape. Every "local" getter converts the value using the host's time-zone rules at that instant, which is why the same program gave three answers in Panel 31.
    
      
    Which strings new Date must read
    
      
    The standard defines one format, a simplified ISO 8601: YYYY-MM-DD, optionally followed by THH:mm, :ss, .sss and an offset Z or ±HH:mm. Date-only forms are UTC; date-time forms without an offset are local time (the map in Panel 32). Anything else is "implementation-specific": engines may guess, and they don't all guess the same, so never rely on it.
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    In real programs
    
    
      
      
        
    • Payments: payment services such as Stripe take amounts as whole numbers in the currency's smallest unit (cents for dollars, yen for yen). Banking code often goes further and uses decimal libraries (decimal.js, big.js) for rates and interest.
      • 
        
    • IDs: X/Twitter's API returns post IDs both as a number and as a string (id_str) precisely because 64-bit IDs lose digits in JavaScript numbers. Databases with 64-bit columns (PostgreSQL bigint) are read into strings or BigInts by most Node drivers.
      • 
        
    • Charts and science: plain floats are fine and fast; graphics and games use them everywhere. The trick is tolerances: "close enough" tests with a sensible size.
      • 
        
    • Showing numbers: Intl.NumberFormat is what web shops, dashboards and admin panels use for prices, percentages, and "1.2K likes". It also has style: "unit" (unit: "kilometer-per-hour") and formatToParts for styling each piece.
      • 
        
    • Dates on servers: store moments as UTC (ISO strings like 2026-11-08T01:30:00.000Z, or epoch milliseconds), and only convert to a zone when showing them to a person. Calendar dates (birthdays) should be stored as plain YYYY-MM-DD text, never as a moment.
      • 
        
    • Date libraries: before Temporal, apps used Moment.js (now in maintenance mode), date-fns, Day.js or Luxon. Temporal grew out of lessons learned there, and polyfills such as @js-temporal/polyfill bring it to environments without it.
      • 
        
    • Repeatable randomness: games with shareable "seeds" and test suites use seeded generators like mulberry32. For passwords, tokens and anything secret, use crypto.getRandomValues or Node's crypto.randomInt, never Math.random.
      • 
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    Common wrong ideas
    
    
      
      
        
    • "0.1 + 0.2 is a JavaScript bug." Every language that uses IEEE 754 doubles gives the same answer: Python, Java, C, Go, Rust.
      • 
        
    • "Floats are random and unreliable." They're perfectly deterministic: the same operations give the same marks every time. They're just not decimal.
      • 
        
    • "Number.EPSILON is the right tolerance for every comparison." Only for numbers around 1. Scale it, or pick a tolerance that fits the job.
      • 
        
    • "toFixed(2) fixes rounding errors." It hides them in a string. Sums made from those strings drift again.
      • 
        
    • "Math.round(x * 100) / 100 always rounds to cents correctly." 1.005 * 100 is 100.49999999999999 (tofixed.js line 8, Panel 11), so it gives 1, not 1.01.
      • 
        
    • "parseInt rounds." It chops at the first character it can't read, so parseInt("3.99") is 3.
      • 
        
    • "BigInt is a faster number." It's slower, and has no decimals. Use it only for whole numbers that need more than 253.
      • 
        
    • "-0 is just a display quirk." It's a real value: 1 / -0 is -Infinity.
      • 
        
    • "A Date stores a time zone." It stores one UTC mark. The zone comes from whatever clock reads it.
      • 
        
    • "getDay() is the day of the month." That's getDate(). getDay() is the weekday, 0 = Sunday.
      • 
        
    • "new Date(\"2026-11-07\") is local midnight." Date-only strings are UTC midnight; add a time (T00:00) to get local midnight.
      • 
        
    • "const stops a Date from changing." It glues the name; setDate changes the object behind it.
      • 
        
    • "Temporal is just a nicer Date." It's several types for different ideas. A PlainDate is not a moment at all.

    bit
