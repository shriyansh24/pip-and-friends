RUST · CHAPTER 19

DUE-DATE STAMPS

Picture: Mabel the librarian holds her ledger and her rubber stamp. On each side stands a little library loan card with a face. The card for Dune is stamped with tick a; the card for Emma is stamped tick static, with an infinity sign under the stamp.

Every loan in Mabel's library gets a due-date stamp. Rust's references get one too: it's called a lifetime. You've been obeying lifetimes since Chapter 8 ("bring it back before the owner leaves"). Now you'll see them as bars on a timeline, learn to write them ('a, said "tick a") when the Compiler can't work them out, learn the three rules that let you skip them most of the time, and put a borrowed reference inside a struct. Seven error codes (three of them new) and one message with no code at all, every one read like a detective. About 120 minutes, with 24 hands-on checks.

  3. Stamps you don't have to write: the elision rules
  4. Bookmarks in a book: structs that hold references
  5. Forever stamps and fine print: 'static, bounds, editions, owning instead
  6. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · EVERY LOAN HAS A DUE DATEa lifetime is a stretch of code, E0597 on a timeline, loans end at their last use, and stamps that change nothing

1

Friday afternoon at the library. A reader borrows Dune, and Mabel brings her rubber stamp down on the loan card.

Mabel says: Every loan card gets a due date. Before it, the card is good: you may read the book. After it, the card is worthless. And one more rule: no card may be due after the book itself leaves my library.

The Professor says: Rust references have due dates too. Rust calls them lifetimes. The Compiler stamps every reference in your program, and checks that no reference is used after its stamp, or after its owner is gone.

Picture: Mabel stamps a loan card with a loud ka-chunk. The Professor explains from the right.

2

In a program there are no calendar days. A Rust "due date" is a place in the code. A reference's lifetime is the stretch of code, from where it is made to where it is last used, in which it is valid.

PART 3 · STAMPS YOU DON'T HAVE TO WRITElifetime elision: three rules the Compiler applies for you, a map of them, and methods (rule 3)

Since Chapter 9 you've written functions like first_word(s: &str) -> &str: a reference goes in, a reference comes out, no stamps. Why didn't those need 'a? Because the Compiler wrote it in for you. Both programs below are the same program to the Compiler.

    

      
WHAT YOU WRITE
first_word.rs

      
WHAT THE COMPILER READS
first_word_full.rs

    

    
Leaving stamps out is called lifetime elision ("elide" means to leave out). Early Rust needed every stamp written. The team noticed the same few patterns again and again, and turned them into three rules. If the rules give every output a stamp, you write none. If they don't, you get E0106.

  


  

    19
    
The Compiler's three rules, in order. Inputs are the parameters; the output is the return type.

    
    
      
  1. RULE 1 · INPUTSEvery reference in the inputs gets its own stamp.
    you write:
fn f(x: &str, y: &str)
the Compiler reads:
fn f<'a, 'b>(x: &'a str, y: &'b str)
    2. 
      
  2. RULE 2 · ONE INIf there is exactly one input stamp, every output reference gets it.
    you write:
fn f(s: &str) -> &str
the Compiler reads:
fn f<'a>(s: &'a str) -> &'a str
    3. 
      
  3. RULE 3 · METHODSIf one input is &self or &mut self, every output reference gets self's stamp.
    you write:
fn f(&self, s: &str) -> &str
the Compiler reads:
fn f<'a, 'b>(&'a self, s: &'b str) -> &'a str
    4. 
    

    

      
      
Mabel says: One book comes in, so a returned book must be that one: rule 2. At a method, a returned book is probably mine, from self: rule 3. Any other case, the Compiler won't guess. Catchphrase: three rules write most stamps for you.

    28
    
    So should a struct hold a &str or a String? A bookmark is cheap but tied to its book. A photocopy costs a little, and you can keep it forever.
    
    
    
      
    BORROW: A BOOKMARK
    
        
    • The struct is short-lived: made, used and dropped while the owner is clearly still around.
    • You're reading through big text and don't want copies (a parser, a search).
    • Price: a <'a> on the struct, its impls and everything that holds it.
    
      
    OWN: A PHOTOCOPY
    
        
    • The struct is stored: in a Vec, a HashMap, returned from a function, kept for later.
    • The data is made inside a loop or function (like format!).
    • Price: a copy (.to_string(), .clone()). Usually tiny.
    
    
    
    
    Picture: two loan cards. The borrow card is titled ampersand tick a str and stamped tick a. The own card is titled String and stamped MINE, with an infinity sign.
    
    owned_bookmarks.rs
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    1. The spot-the-bug program, fixed by owning: page: String (line 2) and page: label (line 9) moves each label into its bookmark. No stamps anywhere, and the bookmarks survive the loop.
      2.

    Under the hood: what the borrow checker really computes
    
    
      
    
        
        
    The Professor says: Lifetimes are entirely a compile-time idea. Here's roughly what the Compiler does with them.
    
      
    
      
    Regions, not braces
    
      
    Inside a function, the borrow checker treats each loan's lifetime as a set of points in the code: everywhere the reference may still be used later. That's why panel 5's last_use.rs compiles: the loan's region stops at its last use, even though the variable card stays in scope until the brace. This behaviour, called non-lexical lifetimes (NLL), arrived with the 2018 edition. Before it, a loan lasted to the end of the variable's block, and many programs that are clearly safe were refused. Older tutorials sometimes show those old errors.
    
      
    Two jobs: inside functions and at their edges
    
      
    Inside one function body, the Compiler works out every region itself; you never write lifetimes for local variables. At the edges of a function (its signature) and inside type definitions (struct fields), it does not look at bodies, so the relationships must be written down, or follow from the elision rules. This is a design choice: changing a function's body can never break its callers, as long as the signature stays the same.
    
      
    How 'a is chosen at a call
    
      
    At longest(&a, &b) the Compiler must pick one region for 'a that satisfies every requirement: &a must be valid for all of it, &b must be valid for all of it, and the result must be valid wherever it's used. If the uses of the result reach past the end of b's life, no region works, and you get E0597 naming b. That's why "the result lives as long as the shorter input" is the right picture.
    
      
    The anonymous lifetime '_
    
      
    '_ means "a lifetime I don't need to name; work it out by the elision rules". You saw it in E0726's help (impl Excerpt<'_>). It's also handy in signatures: fn first(e: &Excerpt<'_>) -> &str. Writing it shows a reader that the type carries a loan, without adding a name.
    
      
    Edition 2024 and use<…>
    
      
    Before edition 2024, a returned impl Trait from a plain function captured only lifetimes that appeared in its bounds, which surprised people in the opposite direction: code that needed to return a loan had to add awkward bounds. Edition 2024 makes it capture every lifetime in scope, matching async fn and trait methods, and adds use<…> ("precise capturing", stable since Rust 1.82) to say exactly which ones. cargo fix --edition adds use<> automatically where the old meaning is needed, which is why panel 32's help shows exactly that.
    
      
    Lifetimes leave no trace
    
      
    After checking, every lifetime is erased. The machine code for longest is the same with or without stamps, and there's no run-time cost or check. A reference in the finished program is just an address (and, for &str, a length).
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    In real programs: where stamps show up
    
    
      
      
        
    • The standard library is full of them, mostly elided. str::trim is fn trim(&self) -> &str: rule 3. str::split returns a Split<'a, P>, a struct holding a loan of your text, exactly like Excerpt. That's why the pieces can't outlive the String you split.
      • 
        
    • Zero-copy parsers. Programs that read big files, such as log analyzers or config readers, often hand out &str pieces of one big String instead of copying each piece. The structs that hold those pieces carry a 'a.
      • 
        
    • Short-lived "views". A struct made just to work on some data for a moment, like a tokenizer over a line or a cursor over a slice, often borrows. Long-lived "records" (a catalog entry, a user, a saved setting) almost always own.
      • 
        
    • Serialization libraries such as serde (Chapter 28) can deserialize into structs with &'a str fields that point straight into the input text, avoiding copies, at the price of keeping the input alive.
      • 
        
    • Threads (Chapter 26) need 'static data, because a thread may outlive the function that started it. That's the T: 'static bound from panel 31, met in the wild.
      • 
        
    • Advice from experienced Rust programmers: start with owned data in your structs, and add borrowing only where measurements show copies matter. Many real programs have few or no hand-written lifetimes outside library code.
      • 
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    Common wrong ideas
    
    
      
      
        
    • "Lifetime annotations make values live longer." They describe; they never extend (panel 7). The owner is dropped at its brace no matter what you write.
      • 
        
    • "Every reference needs an annotation." Locals never do, and the elision rules cover most signatures. You write stamps only where a signature or a struct can't be worked out.
      • 
        
    • "A lifetime lasts until the closing brace." A loan's lifetime ends at its last use (NLL). The owner's life ends at the brace.
      • 
        
    • "'a is the lifetime of x." It's a region that the Compiler picks at each call, one that every reference stamped 'a covers. With two inputs, that's the shorter one.
      • 
        
    • "Give every parameter the same 'a, to be safe." Over-stamping ties lives together that needn't be (panel 16). Stamp only what the result can actually borrow from.
      • 
        
    • "'static means the value lives forever." As a reference, &'static T points at something that will live until the end. As a bound, T: 'static means "contains no short loans", and an owned String qualifies even though it's dropped normally.
      • 
        
    • "When the Compiler suggests 'static, take it." Chapter 9's trap and panel 30: that suggestion is usually a hint that you're returning a loan of something short-lived. Return an owned value instead.
      • 
        
    • "Lifetimes are checked while the program runs." They're compile-time only and erased afterwards.
      • 
        
    • "Structs with references are more advanced, so better." They're more restrictive. Owning is simpler and usually fast enough.

    Lab: run it yourself (Windows, macOS, Linux)
    
    
      
    0. Have Rust
    
      
    If you haven't installed it yet: on Windows, download and run rustup-init.exe from rustup.rs (it may ask you to install the Visual Studio C++ build tools first). On macOS and Linux, run curl --proto '=https' --tlsv1.2 -sSf https://sh.rustup.rs | sh in a terminal. Then rustc --version shows your version; this book used 1.99.1, and rustup update gets the newest.
    
      
    1. Make a loan overdue
    
      
    In a terminal (Windows: PowerShell or Windows Terminal; macOS: Terminal; Linux: any terminal), run cargo new stamps and cd stamps. Paste due_too_late.rs into src/main.rs and run cargo run. Find the three labels. Then fix it both ways from panel 6. Finally try last_use.rs, and add one more println!("{card}") as its last line: now the loan is open while push_str writes, and you'll meet Chapter 8's E0502.
    
      
    2. Stamp a function
    
      
    Paste longest_nolife.rs and read the E0106. Apply the help: by hand. Then write shortest, and a function fn first_two<'a>(list: &'a [String]) -> (&'a String, &'a String). Delete its stamps: does it still compile? (Rule 2: yes.)
    
      
    3. Bookmarks
    
      
    Paste impl_excerpt.rs. Add a method fn last_word(&self) -> &str using split_whitespace().last(). Then try storing several Excerpts in a Vec built inside a loop from format! strings, read the E0597, and switch the field to String.
    
      
    4. Editions side by side
    
      
    Paste rpit_2024.rs and run it. Then open Cargo.toml, change edition = "2024" to "2021", and run again. Now try cargo fix --edition --allow-dirty while the project is still set to 2021. It rewrites your code where 2024 means something different (here it adds + use<> to word_count's return type), and lists the files it fixed. It doesn't change Cargo.toml: then you set edition = "2024" yourself. (--allow-dirty is needed because it edits files that version control hasn't saved yet. Make a copy first if you want to keep the original.)
    
      
    5. Ask the Compiler for more
    
      
    rustc --explain E0106, rustc --explain E0597, rustc --explain E0621 and rustc --explain E0726 print the long explanations with examples. In VS Code with rust-analyzer, you can turn on "inlay hints" for elided lifetimes (the setting is called rust-analyzer.inlayHints.lifetimeElisionHints.enable); the editor then shows the stamps the elision rules filled in, right in your code.
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    Words from this chapter
    
    
      
    
        
    lifetime
    The stretch of code in which a reference is valid: from where it's made to its last use.
    
        
    lifetime annotation (stamp)
    A name for a lifetime, written 'a ("tick a") after the &: &'a str. It describes relationships; it never changes how long anything lives.
    
        
    lifetime parameter
    Declaring a lifetime name in angle brackets: fn f<'a>, struct S<'a>, impl<'a>.
    
        
    owner / loan
    The variable that owns a value (dropped at its brace) / a reference to it.
    
        
    dangling reference
    A reference to a value that's gone. Rust refuses to compile one.
    
        
    borrow checker
    The part of the Compiler that checks every loan against its owner's life and the borrowing rules.
    
        
    non-lexical lifetimes (NLL)
    The rule that a loan ends at its last use, not at the end of its block.
    
        
    lifetime elision
    Leaving stamps out where the Compiler's three rules fill them in.
    
        
    input / output lifetimes
    Stamps on a function's parameters / on its return type.
    
        
    anonymous lifetime '_
    "Some lifetime; I don't need its name."
    
        
    'static
    The lifetime of the whole program run. String literals are &'static str.
    
        
    lifetime bound
    T: 'a: every loan inside T lasts at least as long as 'a. T: 'static: T holds no short loans.
    
        
    local variable
    A variable made inside a function; dropped when the function ends.
    
        
    edition
    A set of language rules chosen per project in Cargo.toml (2015, 2018, 2021, 2024). This book uses 2024.
    
        
    precise capturing, use<…>
    Listing exactly which lifetimes a returned impl Trait may hold.
    
        
    E0597 / E0106 / E0261
    A value doesn't live long enough for its loan; a missing lifetime; an undeclared lifetime name.
    
        
    E0621 / E0515 / E0726 / E0502
    A parameter needs the stamp the result has; returning a reference to a local; a struct's lifetime left out of an impl line; a reading loan still open during a write.
