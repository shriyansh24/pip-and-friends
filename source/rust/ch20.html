RUST · CHAPTER 20

BACKPACKS AND CONVEYOR BELTS

Picture: Mabel the librarian points at a smiling red backpack with two sticky notes poking out of the top, each tied by a dashed string back home; its tag says closure. Beside it, a conveyor belt with three stations, map, filter and a green packing station called collect. A dashed arrow labelled next? runs backwards from the packer, and one yellow item, the number 3, waits at the start.

Mabel has a pile of chores: sort books by page count, keep only the overdue loans, add up the fines, number the shelves. Each one used to need a whole loop. Today she learns two tools that work as a team. A closure is a tiny function you write right where you need it, with a backpack for anything it uses from home. An iterator is a conveyor belt: items ride past stations that reshape them, and nothing moves until the packer at the end pulls. About 160 minutes, with 28 hands-on checks.

PART 1 · THE BACKPACKa function with no name written in bars, type inference that locks in (E0308), a fn can't see its neighbours (E0434), and a closure's three ways of carrying things from home: sticky note, pencil, or the real thing (move)

1

Mabel has three books in a vector and one wish: sort them by page count. Chapter 18's sort() sorts by the first field, which is the title.

Mabel says: I don't want to write a whole fn called page_count_of somewhere far away, just to tell sort one tiny thing: "look at the pages".

The Professor says: Then don't. Write the tiny thing right there, inside the call: books.sort_by_key(|b| b.1). That little |b| b.1 is a closure: a function with no name, written in the very spot it's needed.

Picture: Mabel shrugs at the thought of writing a separate function. The Professor answers from the right.

2

A closure looks like a squashed function. The parameters go between two bars | | instead of brackets, and the body comes straight after.

|x|the parameters, between bars. || with nothing inside means "takes nothing".
x + 1the body: one expression, whose value is handed back. No return needed.
|x: i32| -> i32 { x + 1 }the long form: types written out, braces around the body.
  • No name of its own. A closure is a value, like a number. To call it later, keep it in a variable: let add_one = |x| x + 1; then add_one(4).
  • Types are usually guessed. A fn must write every type (Chapter 4). A closure lives in one small spot, so the Compiler infers (works out) the types from how you use it.
  • Braces when it's long. More than one statement? Wrap the body in { … }, like a function body: the last line without a semicolon is the value.

Mabel says: Bars, then the body. Catchphrase: bars in, answer out.

    PART 2 · THREE KINDS OF BACKPACKFn, FnMut and FnOnce say how often a closure can be called; passing closures to functions with F: Fn and impl Fn; E0594 and E0596; a closure that can run only once; closures in std; and returning closures with impl Fn and Box<dyn Fn> (E0782, E0373)
    

  
    
    12
    
    Every closure automatically wears one or more of three traits (Chapter 17's badges). They describe what the closure does with its backpack, and so how often it can safely be called.
    
    
    
      
    Fnsticky notes only
    Only reads what it captured. Call it as often as you like, even from many places at once.
    
      
    FnMutnotes and a pencil
    Changes what it captured. Call it many times, but one caller at a time, and the caller needs mut.
    
      
    FnOncegives the real thing away
    Hands over something it owns. After one call the backpack is empty, so it can be called once.
    
    
    
    
    Picture: three cards. Fn shows a backpack with sticky notes. FnMut shows a backpack with notes and a pencil. FnOnce shows an open, empty backpack with a dashed outline where an item used to be, and the word USED on its flap.
    
    
      
      
    • They nest. Every closure is at least FnOnce (anything can be called once). One that doesn't give anything away is also FnMut. One that doesn't change anything is also Fn.
      • 
      
    • The body decides, not move. move decides how things get into the backpack. What the body does with them decides which traits it wears. A move closure that only reads is still Fn.
      • 
    
    
    
    
      
      
    Mabel says: Reads, writes, or gives away. Catchphrase: look, change, or hand over.

    PART 3 · THE CONVEYOR BELTan iterator is anything with a next method that hands out Some(item) until None; next needs mut (E0596); adapters are lazy, so a belt nobody pulls does nothing (the unused Map warning); and items travel one at a time, all the way down the belt
    

  
    
    19
    
    Chapter 5 said a for loop works with anything that can be asked "next one, please?". That thing is an iterator. Picture a factory conveyor belt:
    
    
    
    
      
      
    • The source puts items on the belt: .iter() on a Vec, a range like 1..=5, .chars() on a text.
      • 
      
    • Adapters are stations: map reshapes each item, filter drops some into the bin. Each station is itself a new iterator wrapped around the one before it.
      • 
      
    • A consumer sits at the end and pulls: collect, sum, count, or a for loop. It asks the last station for an item; that station asks the one before it, all the way back to the source.
      • 
    
    
    
    
      
      
    Mabel says: The stations don't push. The packer pulls. Catchphrase: nothing moves until the packer pulls.

      PART 5 · THE STATIONSadapters that reshape (map), choose (filter, filter_map, take, skip, step_by), reorder (rev), label (enumerate), combine (zip, chain, flat_map), look ahead (peekable), and slice methods windows and chunks; E0599 when a Vec isn't a belt yet
      

  
      
    27
    
      An adapter takes a belt and gives back a new belt. You can line up as many as you like, and each one only touches the item passing through it. Here is the gallery, grouped by job. (The results are in the two runs below, not typed here.)
      
    
      
      
      StationWhat it does to the belt
      
      
        
      map(|x| …)Reshape: every item comes out as the closure's answer.
      
        
      filter(|x| …)Choose: keeps items where the closure says true; the rest go in the bin.
      
        
      filter_map(|x| …)Reshape and choose at once: the closure answers Some(new) to keep or None to drop.
      
        
      take(n) / skip(n)Only the first n items / everything after the first n.
      
        
      step_by(n)The first item, then every nth one after it.
      
        
      rev()Runs the belt backwards (for sources that can start at the far end, like a Vec or a range).
      
        
      enumerate()Labels each item with its position: (0, a), (1, b)…
      
        
      zip(other)Pairs items from two belts: (a1, b1), (a2, b2)… and stops when either runs out.
      
        
      chain(other)One belt, then the other.
      
        
      flat_map(|x| …)Each item becomes a whole little belt, and they're joined into one.
      
        
      peekable()Lets you peek() at the next item without taking it.
      
        
      copied()Turns &i32 looks into plain i32 copies (for Copy items).
      
            
    
      
    
      Two slice methods make belts of groups: windows(n) slides a window of n items along one step at a time; chunks(n) cuts the slice into pieces of n (the last piece may be shorter).

    Under the hood: what a closure really is, how the Fn traits are chosen, how for desugars, and why chains are fast
    
        
      
        
        
        
        The Professor says: Let's unzip the backpack and look at the stitching.
        
      
        
      
        A closure is a hidden struct
        
      
        For every closure, the Compiler invents a struct with no name you can write. Its fields are the captures: &String for a sticky note, &mut i32 for a pencil, String for something moved in. Calling the closure calls a method on that struct. That's why every closure has its own type (two identical-looking closures are two types), why a closure with no captures takes no space at all, and why impl Fn or a Box<dyn Fn> is needed to name one.
        
      
        How the traits are picked
        
      
        The Compiler looks at the body. A body that moves a captured value out makes the closure only FnOnce. A body that changes a capture makes it FnMut (and FnOnce). Otherwise it's Fn (and both others). Without move, each variable is captured in the gentlest way the body allows. Since the 2021 edition, closures capture only the fields they use: || println!("{}", book.title) borrows book.title, not all of book, so you can still change book.pages meanwhile.
        
      
        Function names are closures too
        
      
        Wherever a closure is accepted, the name of a function with the right shape works: opt.unwrap_or_else(Vec::new), texts.iter().map(String::len) or lines.map(str::trim). A closure with an empty backpack can even turn into a plain function pointer type, written fn(i32) -> i32 with a small f.
        
      
        What for really writes
        
      
        for x in thing { body } becomes, in effect: let mut it = IntoIterator::into_iter(thing); while let Some(x) = it.next() { body }. IntoIterator is the trait for "can be turned into a belt". A Vec<T> turns into a belt of T; &Vec<T> into a belt of &T (that's iter()); &mut Vec<T> into a belt of &mut T. That is the whole reason for x in &v lends and for x in v takes.
        
      
        Why chains are as fast as loops
        
      
        Each adapter is a small struct wrapped around the one before it, with the closure stored inside: Take<Filter<Map<Iter<i32>, …>, …>>. Because every type is known while compiling, the optimiser can inline each next call and each closure call (paste its body in place), until only a plain loop is left. In debug builds (plain cargo run) less is inlined, so chains can be slower there; compare speeds only with --release. Slice belts like iter() also know their exact length, which lets collect reserve the right amount of room up front.
        
      
        One version difference
        
      
        Rust 1.97.0 explains a map on a Vec quite differently. Here is its run of the same vec_not_iter.rs, with a note about trait bounds instead of "is not an iterator" and no into_iter suggestion:

    In real programs: closures as callbacks and settings, iterator pipelines, lines of a file, and parallel belts
    
        
      
          
        
        • Callbacks. Graphical apps, web servers and games register closures to run "when the button is clicked" or "when a request arrives". They're usually move closures stored in a Box<dyn Fn…>, because they must live long after the code that made them.
          • 
        
        • Threads. std::thread::spawn takes a move closure: the new thread gets its own backpack (Chapter 26).
          • 
        
        • Sorting and searching. sort_by_key, sort_by, binary_search_by, max_by_key and retain appear constantly. sort_unstable_by_key is a faster sort when equal items may swap places.
          • 
        
        • Text pipelines. Reading a file line by line is a belt: text.lines().filter(|l| !l.is_empty()).map(str::trim). Chapter 16's search robot walked contents.lines() with a for loop; with this chapter's tools its search becomes one line, contents.lines().filter(|l| l.contains(query)).collect(). Chapter 25 reads files line by line too.
          • 
        
        • Lazy and endless belts. Some sources never end, like (1..) (count forever) or std::iter::repeat. Laziness makes them safe: (1..).map(|n| n * n).take(5) stops after five.
          • 
        
        • Parallel belts. The popular rayon crate adds par_iter(): change one word and the chain's stations run on all your processor cores, because each item travels alone.
          • 
        
        • Async closures (stable since Rust 1.85) let a closure wait for something slow, like a network reply, without blocking; Chapter 27.
          • 
      
        
    
        
  
        

  
        
    Common wrong ideas
    
        
      
          
        
        • "A closure copies the variables it uses." Without move, it borrows them. With move, it moves them (copying only Copy types).
          • 
        
        • "move makes a closure FnOnce." No: move decides how things get in. A move closure that only reads is Fn.
          • 
        
        • "A closure's parameters can be any type, since I didn't write one." The first use fixes the types (E0308).
          • 
        
        • ".map(…) runs the closure on every item right away." Only when a consumer pulls. A belt nobody pulls does nothing.
          • 
        
        • "map runs on all the items, then filter on all of them." One item travels the whole belt before the next starts.
          • 
        
        • "for x in v and for x in &v are the same." The first takes the items and the Vec is gone afterwards; the second lends them.
          • 
        
        • "I can use a belt again after sum or count." Consumers use it up (E0382). Make a new one; it's cheap.
          • 
        
        • "collect always makes a Vec." It makes whatever box you name: Vec, String, HashMap, BTreeMap, HashSet, Result<Vec<_>, _>…
          • 
        
        • "Iterator chains are slow; real programmers write loops." In release builds they compile to roughly the same code as the loop (the Rust book's words), and the optimiser can often remove bounds checks too.
          • 
        
        • "filter(|x| x > 3) should work on v.iter()." The closure gets &&i32 there: write **x > 3, or put .copied() before the filter.
          • 
        
        • "To make my type loop-able I must write map, filter and the rest." Write next; the other methods come free.

    Words from this chapter
    
        
      
        
        
        closure
        A function value written on the spot, with parameters between bars: |x| x + 1. It can capture variables from where it was written.
        
        
        environment
        The variables around the place where a closure is written.
        
        
        capture
        A closure using a variable from its environment: by &, by &mut, or by value (move).
        
        
        infer
        To work a type out from how something is used, without it being written.
        
        
        move
        The keyword that makes a closure take ownership of what it captures (Copy values are copied).
        
        
        Fn / FnMut / FnOnce
        The closure traits: only reads, can be called many times / changes captures, called many times one at a time / may give captures away, so callable once.
        
        
        impl Fn(A) -> R
        "Some closure taking an A and handing back an R", in a parameter or return type.
        
        
        Box<dyn Fn(A) -> R>
        Any closure of that shape, in a box; lets different closures share one type (Chapter 23).
        
        
        iterator
        A value with a next method that hands out Some(item) until None: the conveyor belt.
        
        
        Iterator, Item, next
        The trait, its associated type (what's on the belt), and its one required method.
        
        
        lazy
        Doing no work until asked. Adapters are lazy; consumers do the asking.
        
        
        adapter
        A method that turns a belt into a new belt: map, filter, zip…
        
        
        consumer
        A method that pulls a belt to the end (or until it has its answer) and hands back a result: sum, collect, find…
        
        
        iter / iter_mut / into_iter
        A belt of &T / &mut T / T.
        
        
        IntoIterator
        The trait for "can be turned into a belt"; for loops call its into_iter.
        
        
        while let
        An if let that repeats: while let Some(x) = belt.next() { … } runs the body as long as the value fits the pattern, and stops at the first that doesn't.
        
        
        desugar
        To rewrite convenient syntax into simpler code behind the scenes, like for into while let and next.
        
        
        fold
        The general consumer: a starting value, and a closure that combines the value so far with each item.
        
        
        collect, FromIterator
        The consumer that packs items into a collection, and the trait every such collection wears.
        
        
        turbofish
        The ::<Type> written after a method name to say which type it should produce: collect::<Vec<_>>().
        
        
        zero-cost abstraction
        A convenience that compiles to code as fast as writing it out by hand.
        
        
        error codes (E0308 … E0282)
        E0308 mismatched types (a closure's locked-in type); E0434 a fn can't capture; E0502 a reader while a closure holds the pencil; E0382 used after a move (into a closure, by a FnOnce call, or by a consumer); E0594 assigning inside an Fn closure or through &; E0596 borrowing mutably inside an Fn closure, or a belt not declared mut; E0782 a bare trait used as a type; E0373 a closure that may outlive a borrowed variable; E0599 no such method (a Vec isn't an iterator); E0283 type annotations needed, several types fit; E0282 type annotations needed.
