C++ · CHAPTER 25

BOOKMARKS ON THE LINE

Picture: Wren, the mechanic in orange coveralls, waves hello. Next to her: a book with three bookmarks of different powers, and a little assembly line with an inspection station.

You've kept things in a std::vector since Chapter 8 and walked along it with a for loop. Today we look at the thing that does the walking: the iterator, a bookmark that knows where it is in a container. Some bookmarks can only step forward, some can jump to any page, and some stop working when the book is reprinted. Then we meet the rest of the container family, open the algorithm toolbox (sorting, searching, counting, removing), and build an assembly line of lazy views that does no work until someone asks. We finish by finding the way out of a maze with a queue. About 140 minutes, with 26 hands-on checks.

  1. Bookmarks: iterators, begin and end, and their powers
  2. The container family: deque, list, adapters, span, reserve
  3. Reprinted books: iterator invalidation and safe erasing
  4. The toolbox: algorithms, remove and erase, totals
  5. The assembly line: ranges, projections and lazy views
  6. Finding the way out: a queue walks a maze
  7. Boss level and recap

PART 1 · BOOKMARKSIterators, begin and end, half-open ranges, and what each bookmark can do

1

Wren's workshop has a shelf of manuals. Every manual has bookmarks in it, and Wren has noticed that her bookmarks aren't all the same.

Wren says: In C++ a bookmark is called an iterator: a small object that marks one position in a container. You can ask it "what's on this page?" and tell it "move on". Every standard container hands them out, and the whole algorithm toolbox in Part 4 works through them.

Wren says: What a bookmark can do depends on the book. A vector is one solid block, so its bookmark can jump. A chain of beads has to be walked, bead by bead. Remember the three pictures; they come back all chapter. Know your bookmark's powers.

2

Four pages in a vector. pages.begin() hands out a bookmark on the first page. A star in front, *it, reads the page it marks (the same star you used to follow a pointer in Chapter 13). ++it moves it one page on.

    5. 
    

  


  

    3
    
Where does end() point? Not at the last page. It points at the space just after it, like the inside of the back cover. There's nothing to read there, so never write *v.end(). It's a finish line, not a page.

    

    

      
      
The Professor says: This is called a half-open range, written [begin, end): the square bracket means "includes the first", the round one means "stops before the last". It looks odd for a week and then it's lovely. end - begin is exactly how many there are. An empty container is simply begin == end, with no special case. And a loop that stops at != end never touches a page that isn't there.

    

  


  

  

  

    4
    
The standard sorts bookmarks into categories by their powers. Each one can do everything the one above it can, and more. You don't choose a category: the container decides, because it depends on how the container keeps its things.

Wren says: The snail is forward, the two-way arrow is bidirectional, the rocket is random access. Contiguous is a rocket that also promises the boxes are glued in one row, which is what lets a vector hand its .data() to C code (Chapter 23). Two helpers work for every bookmark: std::next(it, n) and std::distance(a, b) from <iterator>. A rocket does them in one jump; a snail does them one step at a time.

PART 2 · THE CONTAINER FAMILYdeque, list, forward_list, stack, queue, priority_queue, span, reserve and emplace_back


  

    9
    
You know the crate, std::vector: one solid row of boxes. Meet its relatives. Each keeps its things a different way, and that decides what's cheap and what's slow.

    

      
      
std::vector: one solid row. Box numbers are instant; adding at the back is cheap; adding at the front shoves every box along.

    

    

    

      
      
Wren says: A deque (say "deck", short for double-ended queue) is a vector with a door at the front too. A list is a chain of beads: no box numbers, but slipping a new bead in anywhere is one quick relink. A forward_list is the same chain with arrows only one way. Pick the container by what you'll do to it most.

    

  


  

    10
    
Three short demonstrations. Each one uses the trick its container is built for.

    family.cpp
    

      

      

    

    
    
      
  1. Lines 11 and 12: push_front and push_back on a deque are both cheap. A vector has no push_front at all, because it would have to shove every box along.
    2. 
      
  2. Line 18: insert(at, "bee") puts the new bead before the bookmark. Only two neighbours change their arrows.
    3. 
      
  3. Line 21: the bookmark at still marks cat after the insert. Nothing moved, so list bookmarks stay good. Hold that thought for Part 3.
    4. 
      
  4. Line 24: a forward_list can't reach the bead before a bookmark, so it inserts after one: insert_after. It has push_front but no push_back and not even size(): it's built to be as small as a chain can be.
    5. 
    

  


  

    11
    
Sometimes you want a container with fewer powers, so nobody can use it the wrong way. The adapters wrap a container and let you touch only one end.

    13
    
Chapter 23 introduced std::span: a view of a row of boxes that someone else owns, made of a starting address and a count. Because it only needs "a row side by side", one function now takes a vector, a std::array and a C array.

    span_view.cpp

A span only works for contiguous containers: hand it a list and it won't compile, because a chain of beads isn't a row (the notebook's span_list.cpp shows the message).

    

  


  

    14
    
The Professor's cheat sheet. "1 step" means the work stays the same however big the container gets. "n steps" means the work grows with the number of things inside: a thousand things, roughly a thousand steps.

    

      
You do this a lot...vectordequelist

      
        
add or remove at the back1 step*1 step1 step

        
add or remove at the frontn steps1 step1 step

        
insert in the middle, at a bookmarkn stepsn steps1 step

        
read box number i1 step1 stepn steps (walk)

        
find a valuen stepsn stepsn steps

      
    

    

      
      
The Professor says: Textbooks write "1 step" as O(1) and "n steps" as O(n): "big O" describes how the work grows. The star on the vector means "1 step on average": now and then it must move house, which Wren shows next. And here's the surprise: on real machines the vector usually wins anyway, even for some middle inserts, because boxes side by side are what the processor reads fastest. So: start with a vector, and switch only when the cheat sheet and a measurement both say so (Chapter 29 measures). For "find by name", use the map from Chapter 14.

    

  


  

  

  

    15
    
A vector's boxes must sit in one row. When the row is full and you push_back one more, it can't just add a box at the end (someone else might live there). So it moves house: gets a bigger block, moves every number across, and gives the old block back. capacity() tells you how many rooms it has; size() how many are used.

    capacity.cpp
    

      

      

    

    
    
      
  1. The doubling: on this machine (g++ 13's library), each move doubles the rooms: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. Nine push_backs, five moves. Other libraries grow by a different factor, so the exact numbers are not a promise of the language.
    2. 
      
  2. Why doubling? Most push_backs find a free room and cost 1 step. The rare move costs n steps, but it happens so seldom that the average stays at about 1 step: that's the star in panel 14.
    3. 
      
  3. Line 18: reserve(9) books 9 rooms up front. All nine pushes fit, data() (the address of box 0) never changes: moved? no. If you know roughly how many are coming, reserve.
    4. 
    

  


  

  

  

    16
    
One more vector habit. push_back(Robot{"Bolt", 3}) builds a robot, then moves it into the vector's room. emplace_back("Rivet", 5) hands the ingredients to the vector, which builds the robot right in its room. The robot here prints when it's built and when it's moved (Chapter 22's trick).

    emplace.cpp

PART 3 · REPRINTED BOOKSIterator invalidation, seen through the goggles, and erasing while you walk


  

    17
    
Panel 15 left something out. When the vector moved house, what happened to bookmarks in the old house? Picture the publisher reprinting a book into a fatter edition and pulping the old copies. Your bookmark is still tucked into the old copy. The old copy is gone.

    

    

      
      
Wren says: A bookmark that points into a block that's been given back is called invalidated. It isn't empty, it isn't null, and nothing warns you: it still holds the old address. Reading through it is undefined behaviour, so in this book we only ever run that with the goggles on.

    

    

      
The Sanitizer says: Undefined means the program might print the old number, a strange number, or crash, and it can change from run to run. That's why nobody should learn from its output. With my goggles on, I stop the program at the exact read and tell you whose block it was.

    The reprint rules in one table. "Bookmarks" means iterators, pointers and references to elements. end() counts as a bookmark too: any insert into a vector or a deque invalidates it. When in doubt, take a fresh bookmark after changing the container.

    PART 4 · THE TOOLBOXAlgorithm families, transform and copy_if, remove versus erase, unique, partition, accumulate and reduce
    

  
    
    24
    
    Wren's toolbox is the standard library, and <algorithm> plus <numeric> are its biggest drawers: more than a hundred ready-made loops, each tested by millions of programs. Every one takes a pair of bookmarks, begin and end, so the same tool works on a vector, a deque or a list.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
    Wren says: The C++ Core Guidelines put it plainly: prefer a named algorithm to a hand-written loop (rules ES.1 and P.3). A loop says "how"; count_if says "what", and the reader knows at a glance there's no hidden break or off-by-one. Most tools take a test or a recipe as a lambda, exactly as Chapter 24 taught. Name the job, and let the toolbox do the loop.

    ranges_proj.cpp

        2. 
      
      2. Line 18: the three arguments are the container, the comparison ({} means "the usual <"), and the projection &Racer::seconds: "before comparing two racers, look at their seconds". No lambda needed.
        3. 
      
      3. Lines 22 and 23: projections work for searching too. max_element finds the slowest racer; find(racers, "Bo", &Racer::name) finds the racer whose name is Bo. Both return bookmarks, followed with ->.
        4.

    32
    
      Now the best part of <ranges>. A view is a station on an assembly line. It doesn't hold anything: it looks at items from somewhere else as they pass, and it may let them through, turn them into something else, or stop the line. You join stations with the pipe, |.
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
      Wren says: The magic word is lazy. Building the line does no work at all. Only when a loop at the end asks "next item, please" does one item travel down the line, station by station. So a line can even start from an endless supply (std::views::iota(1) counts 1, 2, 3, ... for ever) as long as something downstream says "that's enough". Nothing moves until someone asks.

Then, again and again: take the square at the front, and add every new neighbour to the back, writing down "one more step than the square I came from". Because a queue is first in, first out, every 1-step square leaves before any 2-step square. This is called breadth-first search, BFS for short.

        What you learned in Chapter 25
        
    
          
      
        • KNOW YOUR BOOKMARK'S POWERSAn iterator marks a place: *it reads, ++it moves. Ranges are half-open, [begin, end): end is one past the last, and empty means begin == end. Forward, bidirectional, random access, contiguous: only rockets can + n and [i] (no match for ‘operator[]’ on a list). std::next, std::prev and std::distance work for all.
          • 
      
        • PICK THE CONTAINER BY WHAT YOU DO MOSTvector by default; deque for both ends; list for inserting at bookmarks; stack, queue and priority_queue when only one end should be touched; std::span to read any row. reserve when you know the size; emplace_back builds in place.
          • 
      
        • CHANGE THE CONTAINER, THEN TAKE FRESH BOOKMARKSA vector that moves house invalidates every bookmark, pointer and reference into it (heap-use-after-free). Never add or remove inside a range-for over the same container; use it = c.erase(it) or std::erase_if.
          • 
      
        • NAME THE JOB, AND LET THE TOOLBOX DO THE LOOPcount_if, find, transform, copy_if with back_inserter, sort, unique, partition. Algorithms can't change a size: std::remove only slides (size still 5, 1 2 3 0 3), so erase after it, or use std::erase. accumulate's total has the starting value's type: 0.0 for decimals.
          • 
      
        • NOTHING MOVES UNTIL SOMEONE ASKSstd::ranges algorithms take the whole container and a projection. Views (filter, transform, take, iota, reverse) are lazy stations joined with |; the wrong container is refused at the door (constraints not satisfied). A view or a bookmark must not outlive its container (std::ranges::dangling, stack-use-after-return). ranges::to is C++23 and not in this library yet.
          • 
      
        • A QUEUE MAKES A RIPPLEBreadth-first search: take from the front, add new neighbours to the back with one more step, skip what you've seen. The first time you reach a square is the shortest way there.

    Under the hood: what an iterator really is, how a deque is built, and why views are cheap
    
        
      
        An iterator is usually tiny
        
      
        A vector's iterator is, underneath, just a pointer to an element, wrapped in a class (__normal_iterator in g++'s library, which is the name you saw in panel 8's error). ++it adds the size of one element to the address; *it follows it. A list's iterator is a pointer to a node, a small heap block holding the value plus pointers to the previous and next nodes; ++it reads the "next" pointer stored in the node, which is exactly why panel 21's ++ read freed memory. Iterators are meant to be copied freely and passed by value.
        
      
        Categories are tags
        
      
        Each iterator type announces its category with a tag type (std::random_access_iterator_tag and friends, linked like a family tree), and since C++20 with concepts such as std::random_access_iterator. That's why panel 35's last line talked about one tag "not being a base of" another: the library asked "is a two-way arrow a kind of rocket?" and the answer was no. Algorithms can pick a faster method when the tag says the bookmark can jump: std::distance subtracts for a rocket and counts steps for a snail.
        
      
        How a deque does both ends
        
      
        A typical std::deque keeps its elements in fixed-size chunks, plus a small array of pointers to the chunks (the "index card" in panel 9). Pushing at either end fills the end chunk or adds a new chunk, without moving any existing element, which is why references to elements survive a push_front or push_back. The index card itself may be reallocated, though, and iterators know about it, which is why iterators are invalidated (panel 23). Box i is found with a division: which chunk, then where in it.
        
      
        Why doubling gives "1 step on average"
        
      
        Grow a vector to 1,024 elements by doubling and the moves copy 1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 512 = 1,023 elements in total: fewer copies than elements. However large it gets, the total copying stays under one copy per element, so each push_back costs a constant amount on average. That's what "amortised O(1)" means. Any growth factor above 1 gives this; libraries pick 2 or 1.5 for different trade-offs.
        
      
        Why views are cheap to copy
        
      
        A view such as filter_view holds a reference to (or a small wrapper around) the container, plus its lambda. It holds no elements, so building one is cheap and lines of stations cost nothing until iterated. Two subtleties: a filter_view remembers its first match after the first begin() (so the first search isn't repeated), and because of that, iterating it through a const reference doesn't compile. Pass views by value, or iterate them where they're made.
        
      
        Owning views and std::ranges::dangling
        
      
        When you pipe an lvalue (a named vector) into a view, the view refers to it: ref_view, the name in panel 36's report. When you pipe an rvalue (a temporary, or std::move(v)), the view takes ownership: owning_view, which is how view_owned.cpp survives. Ranges algorithms that return iterators into an rvalue container return std::ranges::dangling instead, unless the range is a borrowed range (such as std::string_view or std::span) whose iterators don't depend on the range object staying alive.

    In real programs
    
        
      
          
        
        • Vectors everywhere. Most real code uses std::vector for nearly everything, with reserve when the size is known. Profilers regularly show that a "clever" list was slower than a plain vector.
          • 
        
        • Queues and stacks. Job queues, message queues, the "undo" stack in every editor, and the call stack itself are queue and stack ideas. Breadth-first search finds shortest routes in games, network hops, and "people you may know" suggestions.
          • 
        
        • Priority queues. Schedulers run the most urgent task next; route planners (Dijkstra's algorithm) always expand the nearest unexplored place next. Both are a priority_queue at heart.
          • 
        
        • Erase-remove in old code. You'll meet v.erase(std::remove_if(...), v.end()) in countless codebases written before C++20. Now you know what it does and that std::erase_if replaces it.
          • 
        
        • Pipelines. Code that reads lines, filters out comments, transforms fields and takes the first N results is a natural fit for views, and because views are lazy, it can stop reading early.
          • 
        
        • Debug iterators. g++'s library has a debug mode (-D_GLIBCXX_DEBUG) and Microsoft's library checks iterators in debug builds. Both stop the program when an invalidated iterator is used, even without sanitizers.
          • 
        
        • Static analysis. clang-tidy has checks that flag hand-written loops which could be algorithms, and std::remove calls whose result is ignored.
          • 
      
        
    
        
  
        

  
        
    Common wrong ideas
    
        
      
          
        
        • "end() points at the last element." It points one past it. Reading *v.end() is undefined (panel 3).
          • 
        
        • "Every container has [i]." Only the random-access ones: vector, deque, array, string. And map's [] means "find or create by key", not a box number (panel 7).
          • 
        
        • "A list is faster than a vector for inserting." Only once you already hold a bookmark at the spot, and often not even then, because walking to the spot is slow (panel 14).
          • 
        
        • "An invalidated iterator is null, or throws." Neither. It holds an old address and reading it is undefined; only sanitizers or debug modes notice (panel 17).
          • 
        
        • "References into a vector are safe from push_back." They follow the same rule as iterators (Boss 2).
          • 
        
        • "std::remove removes." It slides keepers forward and returns where they end; the size doesn't change (panel 26).
          • 
        
        • "unique removes all duplicates." Only neighbours. Sort first (panel 29).
          • 
        
        • "accumulate(v.begin(), v.end(), 0) works for any numbers." The 0 makes the total an int (panel 30).
          • 
        
        • "A view is a copy of the filtered data." It's a station that looks at the original as items pass, so it sees later changes and dangles if the original goes (panel 36).
          • 
        
        • "The pipeline runs each station over all the items in turn." One item travels the whole line at a time, and only when asked (panel 34).

    Words from this chapter
    
        
      
        
        
        iterator
        An object that marks a position in a container; *it reads it, ++it moves it (a bookmark).
        
        
        begin() / end()
        Bookmarks on the first element and one past the last.
        
        
        half-open range
        [begin, end): includes the first position, stops before the last. Empty when begin == end.
        
        
        cbegin() / cend()
        Read-only bookmarks.
        
        
        iterator category
        What a bookmark can do: input, forward, bidirectional, random access, contiguous.
        
        
        std::next / std::prev / std::distance
        Move a bookmark n steps, step back, or count the steps between two bookmarks; work for every category.
        
        
        std::deque
        A double-ended queue: cheap at both ends, still has box numbers.
        
        
        std::list / std::forward_list
        Chains of nodes with links both ways / one way; cheap to insert at a bookmark, no box numbers.
        
        
        container adapter
        A wrapper that exposes only some operations: std::stack, std::queue, std::priority_queue.
        
        
        LIFO / FIFO
        Last in, first out (a stack) / first in, first out (a queue).
        
        
        heap (data structure)
        A row arranged so the biggest element is always in front; not the same thing as the heap memory of Chapter 13.
        
        
        capacity() / reserve()
        How many elements fit before a vector must reallocate / ask for that room in advance.
        
        
        emplace_back
        Build an element directly in the container from its constructor arguments.
        
        
        O(1) / O(n)
        Big-O: work that stays the same / grows with the number of elements.
        
        
        amortised
        Averaged over many operations, as with push_back's occasional reallocation.
        
        
        iterator invalidation
        An iterator, pointer or reference no longer referring to a valid element after the container changed.
        
        
        heap-use-after-free
        AddressSanitizer's name for reading or writing a heap block that was already freed.
        
        
        stack-use-after-return
        AddressSanitizer's name for using a local variable of a function that has returned.
        
        
        algorithm
        A ready-made, named loop from <algorithm> or <numeric> that works through iterators.
        
        
        std::back_inserter
        An output iterator that calls push_back for every value written through it.
        
        
        erase-remove idiom
        v.erase(std::remove(...), v.end()): slide keepers forward, then chop the tail. C++20: std::erase, std::erase_if.
        
        
        projection
        A part of each element a ranges algorithm should look at, such as &Racer::seconds.
        
        
        range
        Anything with a begin() and an end(): containers, views, spans.
        
        
        view
        A lightweight range that looks at another range without owning or copying it (a station on the line).
        
        
        pipe ( | )
        Joins a range to a view, or views to each other, into a pipeline.
        
        
        lazy evaluation
        Doing work only when a result is asked for.
        
        
        constraint
        A requirement a template checks at the door, such as "random-access range". More in Chapter 26.
        
        
        std::ranges::dangling
        The placeholder a ranges algorithm returns instead of an iterator into a temporary.
        
        
        breadth-first search (BFS)
        Exploring in rings of equal distance using a queue; finds shortest paths in a grid of equal steps.
